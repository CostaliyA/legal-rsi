1. Purpose, Scope and Method
1.1 Purpose. The Bank’s Credit Memorandum dated January 22, 2025
recommends approval of a $47,500,000 construction-to-mini-permanent loan to
Harborview Capital Partners LLC, subject to conditions of approval and to
resolution of enumerated risk factors. Our instruction is to convert the executed
Term Sheet dated January 10, 2025 and the approved credit into definitive
documentation, and to report on the material issues and inconsistencies in the
record before the Draft is circulated. This memorandum does that report.
1.2 Method. We read each source document in full. We then re-performed every
material computation in the record rather than accepting the stated result: the
sources and uses, the loan-to-cost, loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratios,
the interest reserve projection, the draw schedule footings, the stabilization
analysis, the guaranty percentages, the acreage and frontage figures, and each
date interval. Where a document stated a result that we could not reproduce, we
have said so and shown our arithmetic. Every proposition in Section 5 is tied to a
specific document and section. Where the record is silent we have said so rather
than assumed a fact.
1.3 What this memorandum is not. We have not re-underwritten the credit, and
we express no view on whether the Bank should make the Loan. Our commentary
on credit metrics is limited to testing internal consistency and to identifying where

3. Executive Summary
The Loan can be documented, but the record as furnished does not support several
of the conclusions the Credit Memorandum relies on, and three of those
conclusions go to the core of the credit rather than to documentation niceties.
They are:
(a) The Project has no borrower equity, and the Loan is oversized against
the Bank’s own limits. The sources and uses allocates one hundred percent of
the $56,700,000 of Total Project Costs to the $47,500,000 senior loan and the
$9,200,000 mezzanine loan, with Borrower cash equity stated at $0 (Draw
Schedule, “Summary” and “Detailed Budget” worksheets; Credit Memorandum
§5.1 and Appendix C). The $8,200,000 land cost is shown in the Detailed Budget as
funded by Mezzanine Loan proceeds, not by member equity. That is
irreconcilable with the Term Sheet (§5.2: land “funded from prior capital
contributions by the Borrower’s members”), the Credit Memorandum (§2.2:
“capital calls from all three members”), and the Organizational Chart (§1.3:
contributed by Ms. Vasquez-Torres “from her personal resources”). On the face of
the sources and uses, the Mezzanine Loan is a land take-out: at closing it repays
whoever funded the land. If that is right, then the “land equity” that the Credit
Memorandum credits at $8,200,000 in its equity analysis (Credit Memorandum §
5.3, § 9.1: $2,140,000 cash + $8,200,000 land = $10,340,000, or 18.2%) is the
same $8,200,000 that the Credit Memorandum’s own sources table has already
counted as mezzanine debt. It is counted twice. True equity is $0, or $2,140,000 if
the equity escrow is funded with genuinely independent cash. See Issue 5.
(b) The Loan exceeds both of the Bank’s leverage limits as set in the
executed Term Sheet, and the prescribed cure does not work. Loan-to-cost is
83.77% against an 80% maximum (a $2,140,000 excess). As-completed loan-tovalue is 65.25% against the 65% maximum in Term Sheet §§ 4 and 6.2 (a $180,000
excess) — a breach the Credit Memorandum does not report, because it measures
against a 70% policy limit that contradicts the executed Term Sheet. The Credit
Memorandum’s cure — deposit $2,140,000 of equity — is presented as an
alternative to reducing the Loan Amount, but on its own it cannot work: because
every dollar of Total Project Costs is already allocated to the two loans, adding
$2,140,000 of equity to the sources column while leaving the Loan at $47,500,000
produces $58,840,000 of sources against $56,700,000 of uses. The equity must
displace Loan proceeds. The two “alternatives” in Credit Memorandum §5.1 and
§10.1.2 are in fact two halves of a single fix: reduce the Loan Amount to
$45,360,000 and fund $2,140,000 of equity. See Issues 1, 2 and 3.
(c) The interest reserve is not adequate, and the record gives three
different answers to how inadequate. The Draw Schedule projects a surplus of
$221,325 on a Daily Simple SOFR assumption of 3.50%; the same worksheet’s own
sensitivity table at 4.35% projects a shortfall of $(365,830); and the Credit
Memorandum projects a shortfall of $(565,000). All three purport to measure the
same thing. The difference is methodology: the Draw Schedule’s month-by-month
accrual is arithmetically sound but is built on a rate assumption 85 basis points

average-balance shortcut overstates total interest by roughly $200,000 at the same

The Draw Schedule does not foot. Draw 9 shows $4,275,000 of components
against a stated draw of $3,275,000, and the component columns aggregate to
$48,500,000 against a $47,500,000 facility. The Detailed Budget’s “Net

but the documents release $3,275,000. See Issues 11, 12 and 13.

requires a deed notice to be recorded by November 29, 2024. The Phase I ESA
(October 28, 2024) records no evidence of recording. The Appraisal (November

2024) searches the public records and does not show it. It is also absent from
the Term Sheet’s list of Permitted Exceptions. If the notice was not recorded,
the NFA determination is out of compliance and the site’s regulatory status is
impaired; if it was recorded, the Title Policy will carry an unlisted exception.
Either way the Bank needs the recorded instrument. The Phase II report and the
NFA letter are not in the record at all. See Issues 21, 23 and 27.

The residential use restriction has not been resolved and the record
does not establish its scope. The Credit Memorandum asserts that fourteen
residential units sit above the restricted 0.38 acres. The Phase I ESA expressly
says that this “requires a detailed overlay” and was not determined. The
number fourteen appears to have been taken from the unit count of threebedroom units. The Appraisal expressly declines to adjust value for the
restriction. See Issue 24.

Two dates are three days apart and cannot both be met. Projected
Substantial Completion is August 31, 2027; the Construction Maturity Date and

thirty-month of a thirty-month construction period and Draw 9 falls after
maturity. See Issue 28.

Borrower’s net worth cannot be reconciled and the covenant would be
breached on day one. Borrower’s only identified asset is the Property,
acquired for $8,200,000, encumbered by a $3,400,000 deed of trust. That
balance sheet yields net worth of approximately $4,800,000, not the
$14,200,000 asserted, and the proposed $12,000,000 covenant would fail
immediately. See Issue 31.

Part A — Capital Structure and Loan Sizing

Issue 1 — Loan-to-Cost Ratio Exceeds the 80% Maximum, and the
Prescribed Equity Escrow Does Not Cure It
Severity: Critical.
Sources: Term Sheet § 3.1 ($47,500,000), § 5.2 (Total Uses $56,700,000), § 6.1
(maximum LTC 80%); Credit Memorandum §§ 5.1, 5.3, 9.1, 10.1.2, 14.1; Draw
Schedule, “Summary” worksheet (LTC Excess / Equity Shortfall $2,140,000).
Finding. The arithmetic is right and the conclusion is wrong. Loan-to-cost is
$47,500,000 ÷ $56,700,000 = 83.7743%, which exceeds the 80% maximum set in
Term Sheet § 6.1. At 80%, the maximum senior loan is $56,700,000 × 0.80 =
$45,360,000; the Loan exceeds that by $2,140,000. To restore 80%, either the
Loan must come down or Total Project Costs must rise by $2,675,000 ($47,500,000
÷ 0.80 = $59,375,000).
The Credit Memorandum’s cure — deposit $2,140,000 into a Bank-controlled
equity escrow — does not by itself change the ratio. Because the sources and uses
allocates 100% of the $56,700,000 to the two loans (senior $47,500,000 = 83.77%;
mezzanine $9,200,000 = 16.23%; Borrower cash equity $0.00%; Credit
Memorandum § 5.1 and Appendix C; Draw Schedule “Summary”), there is no
unfunded line item for the escrowed equity to pay. Adding $2,140,000 to sources
produces $58,840,000 of sources against $56,700,000 of uses — a $2,140,000
surplus — while the Loan remains $47,500,000 and is still fully drawn to complete
the Project. The escrowed cash does not reduce the numerator unless it reduces
the commitment.
The Credit Memorandum presents the equity escrow (condition 10.1.2), a
reduction of the Loan to $45,360,000, and additional evidence of independent land
equity as alternatives (“either: (a) … or (b) … or (c) …”). They are not
alternatives. Option (a) requires option (b) to have any effect on LTC.
Recommendation. Treat the two as a single fix: reduce the Loan Amount to
$45,360,000 and require the $2,140,000 equity contribution. That closes:
$45,360,000 senior + $9,200,000 mezzanine + $2,140,000 equity = $56,700,000.
LTC becomes exactly 80.00%. Obtain the Senior Loan Committee’s written
confirmation that the commitment is reduced (this is a change to the credit
approval and to the Term Sheet loan amount, so it requires Committee action, not
merely documentation). Alternatives worth presenting to the Bank: (i) restate Total
Project Costs upward by adding the omitted costs in Issue 8 — note that adding the
Triangle Commercial Bank payoff alone at its $3,400,000 original principal would

9

bring Total Project Costs to $60,100,000 and LTC to 79.03%, but would require
$3,400,000 of new funding (the same circularity); or (ii) substitute the Appraisal’s
$8,900,000 land value for the $8,200,000 cost basis in the budget, giving Total
Project Costs of $57,400,000 and LTC of 82.75% — still above 80%. Neither
alternative works without new equity.
Draft reference: § 8.1 (testing language and the arithmetic), § 3.1(g) (equity

Issue 2 — The As-Completed Loan-to-Value Ratio Exceeds the Executed
Term Sheet’s 65% Limit
Severity: Critical.
Sources: Term Sheet § 4 (“The Lender’s maximum loan-to-value ratio shall not
exceed 65% on an as-completed basis”) and § 6.2 (65% as-completed and asstabilized); Credit Memorandum § 6.1 and § 12 (maximum LTV 70% for
construction loans; as-completed 65.25% reported as “Pass”); Appraisal § 10
($72,800,000 as-completed; $76,200,000 as-stabilized; LTV 65.25% and 62.34%).
Finding. $47,500,000 ÷ $72,800,000 = 65.2473%. That exceeds the Term Sheet’s
65% cap by 0.25 percentage points, or $180,000 in principal amount ($72,800,000
× 0.65 = $47,320,000). The as-stabilized ratio ($47,500,000 ÷ $76,200,000 =
62.3360%) is compliant.
The Credit Memorandum does not report this. Its underwriting table (¶ 6.1) and its
regulatory section (¶ 12) both state the applicable maximum as 70%, described as
“Bank Policy,” and mark the ratio as a pass. The executed Term Sheet says 65%,
twice. The executed Term Sheet is the operative commercial agreement; a policy
maximum more permissive than the deal-specific maximum does not override it.
The credit file should be corrected so that the Committee approved against the
limit the term sheet actually contains.
Recommendation. The Issue 1 fix cures this as well: at $45,360,000, the ascompleted ratio is 62.31% and the as-stabilized ratio is 59.53%. Conform the
covenant in the Loan Agreement to 65% (not 70%). Ask the Bank to correct the
credit file and to confirm the 70% figure was not the basis of approval.
Draft reference: § 8.2 (both limits stated as 65%, with the computation and the
conflict disclosed).

Issue 10 — Interest Reserve Methodology
Severity: Medium.
Sources: Term Sheet § 3.3 (interest on actual days over a 360-day year); Draw
Schedule Section 2 (monthly interest computed as balance × rate ÷ 12).
Finding. The Draw Schedule computes each month’s accrual as balance × all-in
rate ÷ 12. That is a 30/360 convention. The Term Sheet requires actual days
elapsed over a 360-day year. Over a 30-month period the difference is small (a
few thousand dollars) but it is systematic, and it compounds whenever the reserve
is tested. More significantly, the Draw Schedule assumes each draw occurs at the
start of a month and that no draw occurs in the same month as another; the actual
timing will differ, and because the reserve is funded at closing and accrues interest
on itself, timing errors run in one direction. We recommend the reserve be sized on
a month-by-month basis using actual/360 and the projected draw dates in the Draw
Schedule rather than a level average balance.
Recommendation. Rebuild the interest reserve analysis on an actual/360 basis
keyed to the estimated draw dates (February 2025 through October 2027). Use the
same schedule as the basis for the Interest Reserve Adequacy Test so that the test
and the sizing are consistent. Do not use the average-balance method in the Credit
Memorandum for either purpose.
Draft reference: § 2.4(b); § 4.5(d).

Finding. The Credit Memorandum identifies this correctly: only one lender can
hold a first-priority security interest in the same collateral, and as documented the
two pledges are mutually exclusive as first-priority interests. The Credit
Memorandum’s proposed resolution — senior first, mezzanine second, with a 90day standstill, cure rights, a purchase option, and bankruptcy protections — is the
right answer and is what we have drafted (Draft § 16.3).
The issue is timing and enforceability. The intercreditor agreement is not
executed, and it is the mechanism by which the Bank’s collateral position is
created. If the Loan closes first and the intercreditor agreement follows, the Bank
may hold a first-priority pledge of membership interests from three pledgors while
the mezzanine lender holds a competing 100% pledge; priority then turns on UCC
filing sequence and, for a security interest in a certificated or uncertificated LLC
interest, on control — which for LLC interests means the applicable UCC Article 8
or 9 control mechanics and, in practice, a control agreement or possession of the
certificates if the interests are certificated. Filing sequence alone may not resolve
priority, and the Operating Agreement’s silence on competing pledges
(Organizational Chart § 2.5) means there is no contractual priority.
Note also that the Credit Memorandum’s own description of the Mezzanine Loan’s
collateral is inconsistent with the sources and uses: a pledge of membership

Issue 40 — North Carolina Law Considerations
Severity: Medium.
Sources: Credit Memorandum § 4.3 (“North Carolina is a judicial foreclosure state
and … deficiency judgments following foreclosure are subject to the fair price
confirmation hearing requirements of N.C.G.S. § 45-21.36”); Term Sheet §§ 21.4
(North Carolina law and Durham venue), 8 (Deed of Trust), 16 (remedies); Title
Commitment Note 4 (N.C.G.S. § 44A-10).
Finding. Four North Carolina points require attention before the Loan Documents
are finalized, and we have drafted to each (provisions noted below), but each
should be confirmed by you:
(a) Deficiency after power of sale. Our understanding is that North Carolina has
recognized power-of-sale foreclosure since 1967 under Part 6 of Article 2A of
Chapter 45 of the General Statutes (N.C.G.S. § 45-21.1 et seq.), and the Credit
Memorandum’s reference to “judicial foreclosure” is imprecise. The material point
is that N.C.G.S. § 45-21.36 entitles the mortgagor to a credit for the fair market
value of the property if it is sold for less than fair market value in a power-of-sale
foreclosure, and that this credit applies in the deficiency action. The statute limits
deficiency exposure in practice whenever the collateral is sold below value. We
have drafted the guaranties so that (i) the Partial Repayment Guaranty is a
separate payment obligation limited to the Guaranteed Amount rather than a
guaranty of a deficiency, and (ii) the Completion Guaranty is an independent
obligation to fund completion costs rather than a deficiency obligation. This is the
most important legal issue in the documentation and we recommend it be
reviewed before the Guaranties are finalized; the Interaction between § 4521.36 and a “bad boy” full-recourse guaranty is not free from doubt, and the Bank
should understand that its practical recourse may be to the Completion Guaranty
and the Carry Guaranty rather than to a deficiency judgment.
(b) Usury. N.C.G.S. § 24-1.1 permits the parties to agree to any rate of interest on
a loan in the principal amount of $25,000 or more. The Loan is $47,500,000. The
Default Rate (contract + 5.00%) is not constrained by the 16% general limit, but
we have included a savings clause (Draft § 2.11) as a matter of course.
(c) UCC Article 9 and the pledge. The Membership Interest Pledge must be
perfected by UCC-1 filing against each pledgor (each of the three members) in the
correct jurisdiction of organization, and priority over the mezzanine lender’s
competing pledge should be established by the intercreditor agreement rather
than by filing sequence. See Issue 39.
(d) Mechanics’ lien relation-back. N.C.G.S. § 44A-10 and the title officer’s Note
4; see Issue 19(d).
