## Part E — Construction Schedule and Completion

### Issue 28 — Substantial Completion Is Projected Three Days After the Construction Maturity Date, and Draw 9 Falls After Maturity

**Severity: High.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 2 (Projected Substantial Completion: **August 31, 2027**); § 3.2 (construction period 30 months; target closing February 28, 2025; Construction Maturity Date **August 28, 2027**); § 7.1 (Completion Guaranty requires lien-free completion "on or before the Construction Maturity Date of August 28, 2027"); Appraisal § 3 (projected substantial completion August 31, 2027; as-completed value effective August 31, 2027); Draw Schedule, "Draw Schedule" worksheet (Draw 8 — Substantial Completion / TCO — estimated **August 2027**; Draw 9 — Retainage Release — estimated **October 2027**); Credit Memorandum § 9.6 (acknowledges "only three days").

**Finding.** We verified the date arithmetic, which is internally consistent: February 28, 2025 + 30 months = **August 28, 2027**; + 12 months = **August 28, 2028**; + 6 months = **February 28, 2029**. The problem is not the arithmetic but the sequence. The project is scheduled to achieve Substantial Completion on **August 31, 2027**, three days after the Construction Maturity Date and three days after the date on which the Guarantors must have achieved lien-free completion under the Completion Guaranty. The Completion Guaranty is therefore in default on the day the construction loan matures, on the project's own schedule, with zero float.

The Credit Memorandum ¶ 9.6 recognises the three-day gap and treats the mini-perm period as the buffer. That is a fair observation about the **Loan** — the Loan does not mature until August 28, 2028 — but it does not address the **Completion Guaranty** or the construction-period covenants, which both key off August 28, 2027. As drafted, the Term Sheet requires the Guarantors to guarantee completion by a date on which, on the Project's own schedule, completion has not yet occurred.

Two further timing defects:

- **Draw 8** is scheduled in **month 30** of a 30-month construction period — that is, at the very end, with substantial completion. There is no margin for a single day of delay between the final construction advance and maturity.
- **Draw 9** (retainage release) is scheduled for **October 2027**, after the Construction Maturity Date, which means the retainage is released during the Mini-Perm Period. The Term Sheet describes Draw 9 as a construction-period draw with a "Post-CO" milestone, and the Retainage is security for lien claims arising during construction. Releasing it after maturity is not necessarily wrong, but it should be deliberate, and the Loan Documents should say what happens if retainage conditions are not satisfied until after maturity.

**Recommendation.** (a) Add a tolerance: require completion within **thirty (30) days after** the Construction Maturity Date in the Completion Guaranty (Draft § 13.1), and consider whether the Construction Maturity Date should instead be defined as the date 30 months after the actual Closing Date so that a delayed closing does not compress the construction period (Issue 42). (b) Re-phase the construction schedule so that Substantial Completion is targeted at least 60 days before the Construction Maturity Date; if that is not achievable, the Bank should consider extending the construction period rather than relying on the mini-perm as completion float. (c) Address the post-maturity retainage release expressly. (d) Obtain the General Contractor's current critical-path schedule under the GMP contract before the milestones in Exhibit D are fixed; the dates in the record are the developer's, not the contractor's.

**Draft reference:** § 6.2(b); § 13.1; § 4.3; Exhibit C ¶ 5; Exhibit D.

## Part F — Financial Covenants, Guaranties and Reporting

### Issue 29 — The CC&R Assessment Is Omitted from Net Operating Income and from Both Valuations

**Severity: Medium.**

**Sources:** Title Commitment Schedule B–II ¶ 11(b) (assessment obligations for common areas and district-wide improvements "currently assessed at a rate of **$0.12 per square foot of gross building area per annum**, subject to annual adjustment"); Appraisal § 7 (operating expenses of $1,262,100 — nine line items, none of which is an association assessment; the largest "other" item is $30,038); Term Sheet § 4 and Credit Memorandum § 6.3 (NOI $4,876,800 carried forward without adjustment).

**Finding.** The Property is subject to a mandatory annual assessment of $0.12 per square foot of gross building area under the Brightleaf Historic District Association Declaration. At 287,500 square feet of gross building area that is **$34,500 per annum**, and it is *not* among the Appraiser's nine operating expense line items — the aggregate "Other Operating Expenses" line is only $30,038, less than the assessment itself. The Appraiser either overlooked the recorded Declaration or treated the assessment as immaterial.

The effect is quantifiable:

- NOI: $4,876,800 − $34,500 = **$4,842,300**.
- DSCR at 7.85% on $47,500,000: $4,842,300 ÷ $3,728,750 = **1.2986x** (from 1.31x). Still above 1.25x, but the cushion is thinner than reported.
- As-stabilized value at the Appraiser's own 6.40% capitalization rate: $4,842,300 ÷ 0.064 = **$75,660,900** (from $76,200,000).
- As-stabilized LTV: $47,500,000 ÷ $75,660,900 = **62.78%** (from 62.34%).

None of these changes a conclusion by itself. But a $34,500 recurring expense omitted from NOI is exactly the sort of item that a buyer, an appraiser or an expert will identify in a later dispute, and its existence tells the Bank something about the reliability of the operating expense analysis.

**Recommendation.** Ask the Appraiser for a written confirmation of whether the assessment was considered and, if not, for a revised expense analysis. Add the assessment to the Loan Agreement's Net Operating Income definition so that covenant compliance and the DSCR/Debt Yield tests capture it (Draft § 8.8(c)(v)). Do not represent the as-stabilized value as $76,200,000 in the credit file without a note.

**Draft reference:** § 8.8(c)(v) and its drafting note.

### Issue 30 — The DSCR Covenant Is Tested on a Basis That Cannot Be Satisfied, and There Is No Debt Yield Test

**Severity: High.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 6.3 ("The DSCR shall be tested at the commencement of the Mini-Perm Period and annually thereafter based on actual trailing twelve-month net operating income"); Appraisal § 6.3 (stabilization projected for approximately **May 2028**, nine months after August 31, 2027 completion); Term Sheet § 3.2 (Mini-Perm Period commences **August 29, 2027**); Credit Memorandum § 6.1 (DSCR 1.31x computed on stabilized NOI; stress case 1.04x at +200 bps); § 9.8 (recommends an interest rate cap).

**Finding.** The covenant requires DSCR ≥ 1.25x measured on **actual trailing twelve-month NOI**, first tested **at the commencement of the Mini-Perm Period**, which is August 29, 2027. At that date the Project will have completed construction two days earlier and will not have been stabilized: the Appraiser projects stabilization in **May 2028**, nine months later. Trailing twelve-month actual NOI at August 2027 would reflect a project in early lease-up — possibly very little revenue at all, since certificates of occupancy would only recently have issued. The covenant, as written, is close to guaranteed to fail on its first test date. That is not a credit protection; it is a default waiting to happen, which is useful to neither party and damaging to the Bank if it must decide whether to enforce.

The absence of any rate-insensitive coverage test compounds the problem. The single coverage metric in the Term Sheet moves inversely with SOFR, and the Credit Memorandum's own stress test shows 1.04x at +200 basis points (§ 6.1) — a fail. There is no debt yield test, so the Bank has no measure of the Loan against the property's income capacity that is independent of the interest rate.

**Recommendation.** (a) Test DSCR at the commencement of the Mini-Perm Period on a **stabilized** basis (underwritten NOI adjusted for actual leasing status, or trailing three-month annualized in-place NOI with a lease-up adjustment), and move the first actual T-12 test to twelve months after the final certificate of occupancy. (b) Add a **Debt Yield** covenant of not less than 8.50% (Draft § 8.4), tested on the same dates — on the Appraiser's NOI that is $4,876,800 ÷ $57,373,000, i.e., it permits roughly the current senior balance with a modest cushion. (c) Require an interest rate cap at the commencement of the Mini-Perm Period, not only as an extension condition, given the 1.04x stress result (Draft § 2.14). (d) Retain the curtailment/cash-collateral cure in Term Sheet § 6.3.

**Draft reference:** § 8.3 (with drafting note); § 8.4; § 2.14.

### Issue 31 — Borrower's Stated Net Worth of $14,200,000 Cannot Be Reconciled, and the $12,000,000 Covenant Fails on Day One

**Severity: High.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 6.4 (net worth $12,000,000, tested annually; "current net worth of the Borrower is approximately $14,200,000, based on financial statements provided to the Lender"); Credit Memorandum § 2.1 (same, with "a cushion of approximately $2,200,000 above the covenant minimum"); Organizational Chart § 1.1 (same, "based on the book value of its assets and liabilities as of the most recent reporting period"); Title Commitment Schedule A ¶ 5 (Property acquired at $8,200,000); Title Commitment Schedule B–II ¶ 10 (Prior Deed of Trust securing $3,400,000); Organizational Chart § 1.3 (borrower's only asset is the land; no member has contributed cash other than the land).

**Finding.** We cannot reconstruct a $14,200,000 net worth from any balance sheet disclosed in the record. Borrower's stated asset is the Property, acquired for **$8,200,000**. Its stated liability is the **$3,400,000** Prior Deed of Trust (original principal; the title officer notes counsel's advice that it was "partially repaid"). That balance sheet yields net worth of approximately **$4,800,000** at cost, or **$5,500,000** if the land is carried at its $8,900,000 appraised value. The gap to the asserted $14,200,000 is between **$8.7 million and $9.4 million**, and no document in the record explains it. There is no other asset disclosed; no member has contributed cash beyond the land; and the Borrower is represented to be a single-purpose entity holding nothing else.

The consequence is not merely a documentation problem. The proposed covenant requires net worth of not less than **$12,000,000**, tested annually. On the balance sheet the record actually supports, Borrower would be in breach of that covenant at the Closing Date and at every test date thereafter: post-closing, Borrower's assets and liabilities increase roughly dollar-for-dollar with each Loan and Mezzanine Loan advance, so debt-funded construction does not build net worth. The completion guaranty and the Carry Guaranty exist to address exactly this, but a covenant that is breached from inception is worse than no covenant — it hands the Borrower an argument that the Bank waived it, and it gives the Borrower a basis to resist enforcement.

**Recommendation.** (a) Obtain Borrower's complete financial statements, with a schedule reconciling the $14,200,000 to specific assets and liabilities, and verify the largest items. Do not accept the assertion. (b) Once reconciled, redefine the covenant so that it measures something the Borrower can actually maintain. Options, in order of preference: (i) a net worth test that excludes debt-funded construction in progress and permits the Property to be carried at appraised value; (ii) a **liquidity** covenant on Borrower (e.g., Available Cash of not less than a stated amount) which is more meaningful for a single-asset SPE; or (iii) deletion of the covenant in favour of the guarantor covenants, on the basis that the Borrower is an SPE with no independent capacity to cure. (c) Whichever route is taken, require an initial compliance certificate at closing that demonstrates compliance with the covenant **as defined**.

**Draft reference:** § 8.5 and its drafting note; § 3.1(p); § 5.9; Exhibit I.

### Issue 32 — There Is No Guarantor Net Worth Covenant, and Term Sheet § 15 Cross-References the Wrong Section

**Severity: Medium.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 6.4 (Borrower net worth $12,000,000); § 6.5 (Guarantor liquidity $5,000,000); § 15, final bullet ("Any Guarantor's net worth falls below the minimum threshold set forth in Section 6.4"); Credit Memorandum § 2.3 (net worth profile of each Guarantor; § 2.3.3 analyses guaranty exposure as a percentage of net worth) and § 10.3 (ongoing covenants — net worth for Borrower, liquidity for Guarantors, no Guarantor net worth covenant).

**Finding.** Term Sheet § 15 makes it an Event of Default if "any Guarantor's net worth falls below the minimum threshold set forth in Section 6.4." Section 6.4 sets the **Borrower's** net worth covenant. There is no Guarantor net worth covenant anywhere in the Term Sheet. The cross-reference is a drafting error, and it leaves the corresponding Event of Default without content.

The omission also sits oddly with the Credit Memorandum's own analysis. Section 2.3.3 computes each Guarantor's partial repayment exposure as a percentage of personal net worth — approximately **39.8%** for Ms. Vasquez-Torres ($11,875,000 ÷ $29,800,000 = 39.85%) and approximately **103.3%** for Mr. Osei ($11,875,000 ÷ $11,500,000 = 103.26%) — and expressly notes that Mr. Osei's exposure exceeds his net worth and depends on illiquid real estate. Having identified guarantor net worth as a live risk, the credit document contains no covenant that protects it.

**Recommendation.** Either insert a Guarantor net worth covenant — we suggest 75% of the net worth shown in the most recent personal financial statement (approximately **$22,350,000** for Ms. Vasquez-Torres and **$8,625,000** for Mr. Osei) — or delete the Event of Default. We prefer the former, and would add a requirement that each Guarantor's real estate holdings be valued at not more than a stated percentage of net worth or that the Bank receive an updated appraisal summary of the Guarantors' principal real estate assets annually. If the Bank prefers not to impose a net worth test, the liquidity covenant (Term Sheet § 6.5, $5,000,000 combined) should be increased, since $5,000,000 against a $11,875,000 guaranteed amount is a thin cushion.

**Draft reference:** § 8.7 and its drafting note; § 14.1(p)–(q).

### Issue 33 — The Partial Repayment Guaranty Is Either 25% of the Loan in the Aggregate or $11,875,000 Per Guarantor

**Severity: High.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 7.3 ("a partial repayment guaranty ... in the amount of **25% of the Loan Amount** (25% × $47,500,000 = $11,875,000). The Partial Repayment Guaranty shall be joint and several, meaning that each Guarantor shall be individually liable for the full guaranteed amount"); Credit Memorandum § 2.3.1 and § 2.3.2 ("a 25% partial repayment guaranty (25% × $47,500,000 = **$11,875,000**)" for **each** Guarantor); § 4.3 ("Each guarantor will provide a 25% partial repayment guaranty — 25% of the loan amount of $47,500,000 = **$11,875,000 per guarantor**").

**Finding.** The Term Sheet and the Credit Memorandum describe different guaranties.

- **Term Sheet reading:** a single $11,875,000 obligation (25% of the Loan), joint and several, with each Guarantor liable for the full $11,875,000. Aggregate exposure: **$11,875,000**.
- **Credit Memorandum reading:** $11,875,000 **per guarantor**. Aggregate exposure: **$23,750,000**, or 50% of the Loan.

The difference is $11,875,000 of recourse. The Credit Memorandum's § 2.3.3 analysis — exposure at 39.8% and 103.3% of net worth — uses the per-guarantor figure, which is why Mr. Osei's exposure appears to exceed his net worth. Under the Term Sheet reading his exposure would be 103.3% of net worth only if he were liable for the whole amount as a joint and several obligor, which is what the Term Sheet actually provides.

We note that the Term Sheet's formulation ("joint and several, meaning that each Guarantor shall be individually liable for the full guaranteed amount") is itself internally awkward: if the guaranteed amount is $11,875,000 and each is liable for the full $11,875,000, then the aggregate is $11,875,000, and the sentence's purpose is to make clear there is no pro-rata limitation — not to double the obligation.

**Recommendation.** Confirm the Term Sheet reading — 25% of the Loan in the aggregate, joint and several — and document it with an express sentence: "The Guaranteed Amount is a single obligation of the Guarantors in the aggregate; each Guarantor is jointly and severally liable for the entire Guaranteed Amount, and the Guaranteed Amount shall not be multiplied by the number of Guarantors." Borrower will likely argue for the per-guarantor reading on the strength of the Credit Memorandum; the Bank must not leave the ambiguity in the document. **Draft § 13.3 is drafted on the Term Sheet reading and the point must be raised with the Bank before circulation** — this is a commercial term, not a drafting choice, and we should not settle it for the Bank.

**Draft reference:** § 13.3 and its drafting note.

### Issue 34 — The Carry Guaranty Covers Different Periods in the Two Documents

**Severity: Medium.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 7.2 (Guarantors "guarantee the payment of interest and other carrying costs on the Loan **during the term of the Loan**"; applies after the Interest Reserve is exhausted or is insufficient); Credit Memorandum § 4.3 ("guarantee payment of all carrying costs **during the construction period**, including monthly interest payments, real estate taxes, insurance premiums, and other carrying expenses").

**Finding.** The Term Sheet covers the whole term (construction and mini-perm); the Credit Memorandum covers the construction period only. During the Mini-Perm Period there is no interest reserve and no coverage under the narrower reading, so the Guarantors' carry obligation would terminate exactly when the Project is in lease-up, the interest reserve is gone, and the risk of a payment default is highest — and the Credit Memorandum's own DSCR analysis (Issue 30) shows the coverage margin is thin and rate-sensitive. The Property's real estate taxes and insurance are also "carrying costs" whose scope should be stated.

**Recommendation.** Adopt the Term Sheet formulation and state expressly that it extends to the Mini-Perm Period and any extension, and that it covers interest, real estate taxes, insurance premiums and other carrying costs. The Draft does so (§ 13.2). If the Bank intended the narrower scope, this needs to be resolved before circulation, since it is an expansion of the Guarantors' exposure relative to the Credit Memorandum's description.

**Draft reference:** § 13.2 and its drafting note.

### Issue 35 — Reporting Requirements Differ Between the Term Sheet and the Credit Memorandum

**Severity: Low.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet §§ 13, 21.1; Credit Memorandum § 10.3; § 7.2.

**Finding.** The two lists are similar but not identical. Examples: the Term Sheet requires monthly construction progress reports within fifteen days of month end (§ 21.1) while the Credit Memorandum sets no deadline (§ 7.2); the Term Sheet requires **quarterly unaudited** Borrower financial statements (§ 21.1) which the Credit Memorandum omits; the Credit Memorandum requires a **budget reconciliation** with each draw (§ 7.2) and an **interest reserve reconciliation** monthly (§ 6.2) which the Term Sheet contains only in part; the Credit Memorandum requires a certification of the AIA G702/G703 forms, the Term Sheet does not.

**Recommendation.** Consolidate into a single schedule. The Draft does so at Exhibit F, taking the more demanding of each requirement in each case (which is the Bank's interest, and which is the only reading consistent with both documents). Borrower may push back on items that appear only in the Credit Memorandum (which Borrower has not seen); where an item is genuinely new, the Bank should either waive it or press it expressly.

**Draft reference:** Exhibit F; § 6.10.

### Issue 36 — Extension Conditions Are Not in the Term Sheet, and the Proposed DSCR Test Is Below the Covenant

**Severity: High.**

**Sources:** Term Sheet § 3.2 (one 6-month extension "subject to satisfaction of customary conditions to be determined and set forth in the definitive Loan Agreement"); Credit Memorandum § 11 (proposed conditions (a) through (j)); § 6.3 (1.25x DSCR covenant); § 11(d) (1.15x DSCR extension condition).

**Finding.** The Term Sheet delegates the extension conditions to the Loan Agreement, so the Bank is entitled to set them — but Borrower has not agreed to any of them, and several are material: a 0.25% extension fee (not in the Term Sheet), a 75% occupancy requirement, a 1.15x DSCR, an updated appraisal with a 70% LTV cap (above the Term Sheet's 65%), an interest rate cap, and a pollution legal liability policy.

Two specific defects. First, the **1.15x** DSCR condition sits **below** the 1.25x continuing covenant. Borrower could satisfy the extension condition while in breach of the covenant, or the covenant would have to be suspended during the extension — neither of which is coherent. Second, the **70% LTV** cap in condition (h) contradicts the Term Sheet's 65% maximum; on an updated appraisal the Bank should not accept more leverage on extension than it accepted at closing.

**Recommendation.** Conform the extension DSCR condition to **1.25x**, or keep 1.15x but require a principal curtailment or cash collateral to bring coverage to 1.25x at the time of extension (Draft § 8.3(b) already provides the mechanism). Conform the LTV cap to **65%**. Present the full list to Borrower as the Bank's proposed conditions and flag clearly which are not in the Term Sheet. Expect Borrower to resist the extension fee and the pollution legal liability policy in particular; both are defensible but should be presented as Bank requirements rather than as term sheet terms.

**Draft reference:** § 2.13; Exhibit G and its drafting note under ¶ (d) and ¶ (h).
