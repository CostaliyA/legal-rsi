# 4. DETAILED ISSUES AND RECOMMENDED RESOLUTIONS

## A. ECONOMIC AND UNDERWRITING ISSUES

**A-1 — Occupancy is 56.13%, not 68% (Priority 1).** The Term Sheet (§2.3) states "current occupancy is approximately sixty-eight percent (68%)" and, in the same section, that vacant space totals "13,600 square feet, or approximately 43.9% of total building area." Those two statements cannot both be true: 13,600 ÷ 31,000 = 43.87% vacancy, i.e. **56.13% occupancy**. The appraisal (APP §1 and §3.1) repeats the error and compounds it, stating "68% (21,400 SF of 31,000 SF occupied)" and "vacant area is 13,600 SF (32%)" — the occupied and vacant figures sum to 35,000 SF for a 31,000 SF building. The rent roll is correct: Bay 1 (3,800) + Bay 3 (3,800) + Suite A (9,800) = **17,400 SF occupied**, 13,600 SF vacant, 3 of 5 units. The appraisal's weighted average in-place rent of \$19.40/SF is also derived from the erroneous 21,400 SF denominator; the correct figure is \$23.86/SF (\$415,100 ÷ 17,400).

*Effect.* Every covenant, budget, lease-up projection and stabilization assumption in the record that was calibrated to 68% occupancy is calibrated to a number that is 11.9 percentage points too high. The appraiser's as-stabilized value assumes 95% occupancy and a 18–24 month lease-up; measured from 56.13% rather than 68%, the required absorption is materially greater.

*Resolution.* The Mortgage does not state an occupancy percentage (deliberately — the instrument should not hard-code a figure that the record gets wrong). Exhibit D states 56.13% and 17,400 SF. Recommend: (i) the closing rent roll be re-certified on the correct basis; (ii) the appraisal be reviewed with the appraiser as to the occupancy error and its effect, if any, on the 6.25% as-is capitalization rate and the \$18,200,000 as-is value — if the error is confined to the summary narrative the value may be unaffected, but the client should not accept the report without asking; and (iii) if the client intends any occupancy-based covenant (e.g. a lease-up benchmark or a guaranty burn-off), it must be drafted from 56.13%.

**A-2 — The DSCR covenant cannot be satisfied (Priority 1).** This is the most serious issue in the transaction. Section 7.1 of the Term Sheet sets a 1.25x minimum DSCR, tested quarterly from month 24; Section 11.1(i) makes two consecutive quarters below 1.25x an Event of Default. Section 3.9 of the Term Sheet ends the interest-only period at month 24 and starts 25-year amortization at month 25. So the first test dates coincide exactly with a step-up in debt service. Computed at the SOFR floor (3.00% + 2.85% = 5.85%), the numbers are:

| Measure | Amount |
|:---|---:|
| Monthly interest-only payment | \$71,906.25 |
| Annual debt service, interest-only | \$862,875 |
| Monthly P&I, 25-year amortization at 5.85% | \$93,686.61 |
| Annual debt service, first amortizing year | \$1,124,239 |
| NOI required for 1.25x on interest-only debt service | \$1,078,594 |
| NOI required for 1.25x on amortizing debt service | \$1,405,299 |
| Borrower's "stabilized best case" NOI (RR, Vacancy Schedule) | \$622,772 |
| Implied DSCR on interest-only debt service | **0.72x** |
| Implied DSCR on amortizing debt service | **0.55x** |
| Borrower's "realistic" NOI at the April 15, 2027 test date | \$314,782 |
| Implied DSCR on interest-only / amortizing debt service | **0.37x / 0.28x** |

Put differently: on the borrower's own stabilized projections, the loan would have to be approximately **\$6.5 million** (amortizing) or **\$8.5 million** (interest-only) to test at 1.25x. The loan is \$14,750,000. The borrower's own workbook flags the result in terms: "DOES NOT MEET 1.25x COVENANT."

*Resolution (client decision required).* The Mortgage implements the Term Sheet faithfully (**Sections 7.8(a), 12.1(i)**) because we cannot unilaterally rewrite a covenant the client has committed to. But I recommend we put four options to Marcus: (i) retain the covenant and accept that default is effectively pre-ordained at month 24 (not recommended — a covenant that cannot be met is a covenant that will be waived, and waiver creates lender-liability and documentation exposure); (ii) extend the interest-only period to month 36 and begin testing at month 36 to permit lease-up (addresses timing but not the arithmetic); (iii) retain the covenant and require a DSCR cure deposit, cash sweep trigger at 1.10x, or an additional interest/DSCR guaranty sized to the gap (**G-8**); or (iv) re-size the loan. This should not be left to closing.

**A-3 — As-is LTV of 81.04% requires a documented credit exception (Priority 2).** \$14,750,000 ÷ \$18,200,000 = 81.04%; the as-stabilized LTV of 62.77% is the more comfortable number but rests on a **prospective** value subject to a hypothetical condition (renovation complete plus 95% occupancy). Loan-to-cost is 70.41% (\$14,750,000 ÷ \$20,950,000) and borrower equity is 29.59% of total project cost. As you anticipated in your closing note, an 81% as-is LTV is outside conventional commercial real estate limits. *Resolution:* obtain written confirmation from Marcus that loan committee or the board has approved the exception, and place the confirmation in the closing file; if the approval is conditional, the conditions need to be reflected in the Loan Documents. I have flagged this in the Mortgage's recitals indirectly (the Loan is made "on the terms and subject to the conditions set forth in the Term Sheet") but a credit exception is not a document term and should be evidenced separately.

**A-4 — Sources and uses do not balance; identified shortfall of at least \$352,075 (Priority 1).** The Term Sheet (Exhibit B) funds the renovation as \$2,250,000 holdback plus \$900,000 borrower equity, and the purchase price (Section 3.2) as \$17,800,000. Its "Total Project Cost" (Section 3.4) is \$20,950,000, exactly equal to the \$14,750,000 loan plus the \$6,200,000 equity commitment. But total project cost excludes closing costs and the \$150,000 TI/LC reserve that Section 6.3 requires to be deposited **at closing**. Working backwards: purchase price \$17,800,000 less Initial Advance \$12,500,000 = \$5,300,000 to come from equity; equity commitment \$6,200,000 less \$5,300,000 = \$900,000, which is exactly the "Borrower Equity for Renovation" in the Term Sheet's Exhibit B. The equity is therefore fully committed with nothing left for closing costs or the reserve. Known closing costs alone (origination fee \$147,500 plus mortgage recording tax \$54,575) are \$202,075, and adding the \$150,000 reserve gives **\$352,075** before any title premium, survey, appraisal, ESA, legal fees or documentary stamps on the deed.

*Resolution.* Require a certified closing statement and a sources-and-uses schedule as a condition precedent (already contemplated by Term Sheet §12.1(m), which requires "evidence of the funding" of the \$6,200,000), and require written evidence that Borrower will fund the shortfall from additional equity or member capital contributions. Draft consequence: **Section 10.6** obliges Mortgagor to fund all costs in excess of the Renovation Holdback from its own funds, and **Section 7.4(g)** requires adequate capitalization — but neither cures a shortfall at the closing table.

**A-5 — The borrower's debt service arithmetic is wrong (Priority 3).** The rent roll's Vacancy Schedule states a monthly P&I payment of \$93,500 and annual interest-only debt service of \$863,375. The correct figures are **\$93,686.61** (25-year, 5.85%) and **\$862,875** (5.85% on \$14,750,000). The errors are small (\$186.61 per month; \$500 per year) and run in opposite directions, but they are symptomatic of a model that has not been independently checked, which matters because the client is being asked to rely on the same model for the lease-up and NOI projections at **A-2**.

**A-6 — The renovation draw schedule does not foot to the budget (Priority 2).** The Draw Schedule tab totals **\$905,000** of Borrower Equity against a \$900,000 commitment (leaving a negative "\$-5,000 remaining" in month 14) and **\$3,155,000** of cumulative cost against a \$3,150,000 budget, while its own TOTAL row asserts \$900,000 and \$3,150,000. Separately, the Budget Detail tab footnote says the GC Contract "exceeds Lender Holdback of \$2,250,000 by \$600,000" — but \$150,000 of the holdback is allocated to soft costs outside the GC Contract, so the shortfall to be funded by equity on the hard-cost scope is **\$750,000** (\$2,850,000 less \$2,100,000), not \$600,000. Neither error changes the borrower's total obligation, but both obscure the funding sequence. I have conformed **Exhibit C** to the \$2,250,000/\$900,000/\$3,150,000 figures and noted the discrepancy on the face of the exhibit, and **Section 10.2(d)** imposes a pro-rata funding test so that Lender does not fund ahead of equity.

**A-7 — The draw schedule does not model the 10% retainage, and the equity tail is thin (Priority 2).** Two mechanical problems. First, Term Sheet §8.4 requires Lender to retain **ten percent (10%) of each disbursement** from the Renovation Holdback until substantial completion. The RBS Draw Schedule does not model that retainage: its lender draws sum to exactly \$2,250,000 with nothing withheld. If retainage is taken as the Term Sheet requires, approximately **\$212,000** will be held back by month 11 and a full **\$225,000** by substantial completion, none of which reaches Borrower when the RBS schedule assumes it does. Second, the equity tail is extremely thin: under the RBS schedule only **\$50,000** of Borrower Equity remains unspent at month 11 and **\$20,000** at month 12, against **\$130,000** and **\$65,000** of undrawn holdback. Combining the two, Borrower would need roughly \$160,000 more working capital in months 10–12 than the schedule shows. Cumulatively the lender's share of draws ramps from 25% at month 1 to 71.43% only at month 14 (so equity is funded ahead of entitlement throughout, which is favourable to Lender), but the thin tail means any cost overrun in the last third of the Project lands immediately on Borrower.

*Resolution.* The pro-rata funding test in **Section 10.2(d)** is largely satisfied by the schedule as designed and therefore is not the problem; the problem is that the schedule understates Borrower's interim cash need. **Section 10.5(f)** permits Lender to withhold a Draw if the undisbursed holdback plus the unfunded equity is insufficient to complete, and **Section 10.6** requires Borrower to fund overruns. Recommend: require Borrower to demonstrate, before the first Draw, that it has at least the full \$900,000 of equity available (not merely committed) and the capacity to fund the retainage gap, and require a re-cast draw schedule that models retainage.

**A-8 — Balloon and rollover risk at maturity (Priority 3).** The Loan amortizes over 25 years but matures at month 60, so approximately **\$13,895,165** will be outstanding at the April 15, 2030 Maturity Date (36 amortizing payments having been made). Refinance risk is compounded by rollover: the Tidemark lease (9,800 SF, 31.6% of the building and the anchor cash flow) expires **March 31, 2029**, roughly twelve and a half months before maturity, and the Lowcountry Provisions lease expires December 31, 2027. At maturity, absent renewal, the lender is looking at a substantially vacant building. Recommend the client consider a maturity-extension/rollover reserve or a cash-management trigger in the final 24 months; neither is in the Term Sheet, so this is a credit recommendation only.

## E. PREPAYMENT AND YIELD MAINTENANCE ISSUES

**E-1 — The formula in the Markup Notes produces a zero premium; I have drafted a different formula (Priority 1).** Your §5 draft provides that the premium equals the positive excess of "(A) the present value of all remaining scheduled monthly payments of interest and principal from the prepayment date through the last day of the Yield Maintenance Period … over (B) the outstanding principal amount being prepaid," floored at zero. Applied to this Loan, the result is not a small premium — it is a large negative number:

| Element (illustrative prepayment at month 30; 18 months remaining; 25-year amortization) | Amount |
|:---|---:|
| Outstanding principal at month 30 | \$14,617,715 |
| Present value of the 18 remaining scheduled P&I installments, discounted at a 4.00% Treasury rate | \$1,634,124 |
| Excess of (A) over (B) | **\$(12,983,591)** — i.e. zero premium |

The defect is structural, not arithmetical: with 25-year amortization and a 60-month maturity, **there is no scheduled balloon payment at the end of the Yield Maintenance Period**, so the stream of "remaining scheduled monthly payments" never includes repayment of principal. A formula of this kind works only for a fully amortizing loan or where the scheduled balance at the end of the yield maintenance period is included as a deemed final payment. (Equivalently: adding the present value of the scheduled month-48 balance — \$14,617,715 ÷ (1 + 4%/12)^18 = \$13,767,819 — gives a make-whole of \$784,228, which is a different and larger measure than the Term Sheet contemplates.)

**E-2 — What I drafted, and why (Priority 1).** **Section 16.2** implements the Term Sheet's own formulation, which is both faithful to the binding commitment and mathematically determinate: the Yield Maintenance Premium is the greater of (a) **1.00%** of the principal prepaid, and (b) the present value, discounted monthly at the Treasury Rate, of the excess of interest at the **Note rate** over interest at the **Treasury Rate plus 50 basis points**, in each case on the principal prepaid for the remainder of the Yield Maintenance Period, floored at zero. For the same illustrative month-30 prepayment with a 4.00% Treasury rate, the Term Sheet formulation produces **\$286,840**, against a 1% floor of \$146,177 — so clause (b) governs, as intended. I have also defined the Treasury Rate precisely (H.15 Treasury Constant Maturities, maturity closest to but not exceeding the end of the Yield Maintenance Period, linear interpolation if there is no exact match, determined two Business Days before prepayment), the discounting convention, the zero floor, and Lender's calculation mechanics with a written calculation delivered five Business Days before prepayment.

**E-3 — Where the 50 basis points go changes the number by about \$212,000 (Priority 1 — client decision).** You asked me to add the 50 basis points to the **loan rate** and confirm with Marcus. That reading is not what the Term Sheet says: §4.2(b) applies the spread to the *reinvestment* leg ("interest that would accrue … at the treasury rate plus fifty (50) basis points"), which is also the conventional construction. The economic difference is material: on the same illustrative facts, adding 50 bps to the loan rate (your reading) produces **\$499,314**, versus **\$286,840** under the Term Sheet reading — a difference of **\$212,474** on that one prepayment. I have drafted the Term Sheet reading. **E-4 — The 1% floor must survive (Priority 1).** Your draft formula has a zero floor and no minimum. The Term Sheet (§4.2) says "the greater of (a) one percent (1.00%) of the outstanding principal balance … or (b) [the differential]." Deleting the floor would be materially inconsistent with the binding commitment. **Section 16.2(a)** retains it. Note the interaction with **E-2**: because clause (b) is floored at zero and clause (a) is a hard 1%, a Borrower prepaying when Treasury rates have risen still pays 1% — which is the Term Sheet's bargain.

**E-5 — Other prepayment mechanics (Priority 2).** I have drafted: an absolute lockout for months 1–24 (Term Sheet §4.1); whole-loan-only prepayment during the Yield Maintenance Period with 30 days' notice and payment on a Payment Date; partial prepayment in the open period on 15 days' notice, in minimum increments of \$500,000 (the increment is an addition — **H-5**); application of partial prepayments to installments of principal in inverse order of maturity without change to the amortization schedule or Maturity Date; and the statement that the premium is bargained-for consideration, not a penalty, with a waiver of the penalty argument (**Section 16.7**). **G-4 — Late charge and payment cure interact awkwardly (Priority 3).** The Late Charge accrues if an installment is not received within **10 calendar days** (Term Sheet §3.11; **Section 15.4**), while the payment default cure period is **5 Business Days** (Term Sheet §11.2(a); **Section 12.2(a)**). A payment received on day 7 or 8 is in default (cure expired) but incurs no late charge; a payment received on day 9 or 10 is in default and incurs a late charge. **Section 12.2(a)** notes that the Late Charge may be assessed independently and does not extend the cure period. The interaction is workable but inelegant; if the client wants the two periods aligned, we can conform both to 10 calendar days. Separately, a 5% late charge is at the upper end of what South Carolina courts are likely to enforce as a reasonable estimate of administrative cost, and **Section 15.4** includes the recitals (difficulty of ascertainment, reasonable estimate, not a penalty) intended to support enforceability; **Section 15.8** provides an interest-rate savings clause. I would not expect difficulty at 5% but the recitals should stay.

**G-5 — Default rate of 10.85% (Priority 3).** Applicable Rate 5.85% plus 5.00% = **10.85%**. **Section 15.8** contains a full interest-rate limitation and savings provision, which I recommend retaining regardless: it protects against the risk that the Default Rate (which continues during the pendency of a bankruptcy) or the combination of default interest and late charges is held to exceed an applicable ceiling. I have not opined on the South Carolina usury ceiling in this memorandum; a short confirmation against S.C. Code Ann. Title 34, Chapter 31 should be added to the research list alongside **F-2**.

**G-6 — "Full recourse personal guaranty" is a misdescription (Priority 2).** Term Sheet §5.5 is headed "**Guaranty**" and opens with the statement that Guarantor "shall execute and deliver a full recourse personal guaranty," then immediately structures it as (a) a non-recourse carve-out guaranty, (b) a completion guaranty and (c) an interest-shortfall guaranty limited to 14 months. Only the carve-out limb is "full recourse," and only on a bad-boy event; the rest is limited. The Mortgage reflects the actual structure (**Section 18.14**, **Section 7.18**) rather than the heading. This matters for credit: the personal guaranty does not support debt service generally, so the lender's real protection is the Property, the completion guaranty and a 14-month interest backstop against an 81% as-is LTV and a covenant that cannot be met.

**G-7 — The completion guaranty needs a cost-of-completion mechanism (Priority 2).** Term Sheet §5.5(b) guarantees completion "within the approved renovation budget and by the Substantial Completion Deadline." The Mortgage cross-references it (**Section 10.6**, **Section 7.18**) and gives Lender the right to complete the Project and charge the cost (**Section 10.10**). *Action:* confirm that the Completion Guaranty, when delivered, covers budget overruns as well as physical completion, and that it is not discharged by Lender's own completion (a common carve-out in guarantor-favourable forms).

**G-8 — The ten-month unguarded gap (Priority 1).** The payment guaranty covers an interest shortfall for the first 14 months. Substantial Completion is due at month 14. The first DSCR test is at month 24. Between month 15 and month 24 there is neither a guaranty of debt service nor a tested covenant — and, on the borrower's own projections, that is the period in which Suite B is only just commencing rent (projected February 1, 2027, which is 2.5 months before the April 15, 2027 test date the borrower assumes). Recommend to Marcus that the interest-shortfall guaranty be extended through at least the first DSCR test date (and preferably through the earlier of stabilization or month 36), and that a DSCR/interest reserve be funded at closing from the equity contribution — which, per **A-4**, also has no source at present.
