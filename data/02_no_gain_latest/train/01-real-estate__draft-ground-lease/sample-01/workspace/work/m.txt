Issue 1 — The Premises Description Does Not Close, and Two Descriptions
Are in Circulation
Severity: Critical. Sources: Title Commitment, Schedule A Section 5 and
Exhibit A; Survey, Sections 1 and 4; Term Sheet Sections 2 and 13.
Record facts. The Title Commitment describes the land as “3.2 acres (139,392
square feet), more or less,” and its Exhibit A attaches a metes-and-bounds
description with courses of 580.00 feet (along Pier Street), 240.40 feet and 239.60
feet (along the bulkhead line), 582.50 feet (along the southern boundary), and


                                             Page 4 of 31
                        Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


480.20 feet (along Harbor Avenue), said to be “As shown on that certain
ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey prepared by Calloway Land Surveyors LLC, J.
Michael Ferreira, P.L.S., Connecticut License No. 12847, dated April 20, 2025, and
last revised May 15, 2025, Drawing No. CLS-2025-0321.” The Survey in the record
is a different instrument: prepared by Thomas R. Pellegrino, P.L.S., License
No. PLS-008741, Job No. CLS-2025-0417, plat dated April 28, 2025, and it
describes a rectangle of 435.22 feet by 320.47 feet with bearings of S 78°15’30” E
and S 11°44’30” W respectively.
Analysis. We computed the area enclosed by the Title Commitment’s own courses
using those bearings and distances: approximately 279,535 square feet, or
6.42 acres — almost exactly double the 139,392 square feet stated in the same
document, in the Term Sheet, in the Resolution, in the Phase II ESA, and in the
Survey. The two descriptions are not variants of the same parcel: they have
different bearings (72°15’30” versus 78°15’30”), different dimensions, different
surveyors, and different drawing numbers. They cannot both be right, and the Title
Commitment is wrong on its face if the Survey is right. Three consequences follow.
First, a lease that demises a parcel by a description that does not match the survey
of record is a lease whose premises are undefined — the defect that most often
produces a title exception that the lender will not accept. Second, the Survey and
the title description cannot both support the ALTA certification required by
Bayside’s condition 6(d). Third, the recited area drives the $20.45 per square foot
rent figure in Term Sheet Section 5; if the parcel were 279,535 square feet, the
rent would be $10.19 per square foot, although the dollar rent itself would be
unaffected. (As a related and much smaller point, the Survey’s own courses
compute to 139,475 square feet, 83 square feet more than the stated 139,392 — a
0.06% variance that is immaterial but that confirms the geometry should be
confirmed by the surveyor before recording.)
Position taken. Section 2.2 provides that the Survey description governs, that the
Title Commitment’s Exhibit A is erroneous, that Exhibit A to the Lease consists
exclusively of the Survey description, and that receipt of title policies reflecting the
corrected description is a condition to the Lease Commencement Date. The stated
area controls for all purposes. A joint boundary update is required before the first
permit application. Exhibit A carries the surveyor’s name, license number, and job
number, and expressly disclaims the Ferreira drawing.
Fallback. If the title company will not amend Exhibit A before issuance, accept an
insured-over description but require an ALTA 25-06 (same as survey) endorsement
and a surveyor’s affidavit, and shift the cost and risk of any later correction to
Landlord.
Open item. Obtain the Ferreira drawing CLS-2025-0321 and have the surveyor of
record confirm in writing which drawing and which description govern, and
whether the 480-linear-foot bulkhead figure is a reference to a longer frontage
than the surveyed eastern boundary. See Part VIII.

Issue 2 — The Reverter Right Is the Deal, and It Must Be Repriced
Severity: Critical. Sources: Term Sheet Section 9; Instructions Section 2;
Resolution Section 2(a) and Section 4.


                                        Page 5 of 31
                        Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


Record facts. If Milestone 1 or Milestone 3 is missed, the Term Sheet permits the
Authority to terminate on notice; Tenant has 180 days to cure; and upon expiration
of that period the Lease “shall automatically terminate,” Tenant “shall forfeit the
security deposit,” and “[a]ll improvements… shall immediately revert to and
become the sole property of the Landlord… without any payment, compensation, or
reimbursement to Tenant.” The Resolution requires “reverter rights in favor of the
Authority in the event of Tenant’s failure to achieve such milestones,” and
prohibits the Executive Director from approving any “material” deviation from the
Term Sheet without Board action.
Analysis. The exposure is not theoretical. The record’s total hard cost budget is
$352,000,000 (Bayside Term Sheet Section 3). The record contains no Phase
1/Phase 2 allocation, but the 28-story residential tower with podium retail and
parking is plainly the dominant component; at 60–75% of hard cost, the Phase 1
improvements that would be at risk at Milestone 3 are approximately $211–264
million, consistent with your $200–300 million estimate. Against that, the
Authority’s actual harm from a missed milestone is measured in months of delayed
ground rent during a period when, under Section 3 of the Term Sheet, no Base
Rent is yet payable. The forfeiture is therefore not a measure of the Authority’s
loss; it is a transfer of your capital to the Authority. That is the profile of a penalty,
and it is also, more immediately, an obstacle no construction lender will
underwrite (Part VI addresses the legal question you raised).
Two structural defects compound the problem. First, the Term Sheet makes
termination automatic, which leaves no room for the lender protections in the
same section. Second, the forfeiture extends by its terms to “any earlier
termination” (Term Sheet Section 18), which would capture terminations caused
by the Authority’s own default, by casualty, or by condemnation — while Section
17(d) of the same Term Sheet says that in a condemnation you are entitled to the
portion of the award attributable to the improvements. Those provisions cannot
both be given effect.
Position taken. Article 7 keeps the reverter the Authority bargained for, which is
important for Board approval, and prices it: (a) no automatic termination —
Landlord terminates only by notice after all cure periods expire (Section 7.6(b));
(b) 180-day Tenant cure plus an independent 180-day Leasehold Mortgagee cure,
with an express right for the mortgagee to install a replacement developer
(Sections 7.5, 7.6(c), 10.3(c)); (c) Reverter Compensation equal to the greater of
appraised Fair Market Value of the Improvements or Net Hard Costs, net of
capped Landlord Damages (Section 7.7); (d) Landlord Damages limited to re-
marketing costs, out-of-pocket costs, and no more than 24 months of Base Rent,
with an express mitigation duty and no consequential damages; (e) no transfer of
possession until compensation is paid or escrowed, with a rescission right if it is
not (Section 7.7(e)); (f) the security deposit is drawable only to the extent of
Landlord Damages, not in full (Section 7.9); (g) a reformation clause confirming
the parties’ intent that the reverter is not a penalty (Section 7.7(f)); and (h) an
enumeration of circumstances in which no reverter occurs at all (Section 7.10).
Fallback. If the Authority will not accept a “greater of” test, accept Net Hard Costs
alone (your option (b) in the Instructions) provided that (i) Landlord Damages are


                                        Page 6 of 31
                       Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


capped, (ii) the mortgagee cure and New Lease rights are preserved, and (iii) the
security deposit is not additionally forfeited. If the Authority insists on formula
caps, prefer a stated percentage of hard cost (e.g., 90%) over an appraised value
determined by an appraiser the Authority selects.
Board approval. The Resolution prohibits material deviations from the Term Sheet
without Board action, and reverter compensation is the paradigm of a material
deviation. Expect the Authority’s counsel to require a new or supplemental
resolution. We have drafted Section 18.18 to require Landlord to submit the Lease
for approval; see log Issue 12 and Part VII.

Issue 3 — The Reverter Cannot Be Reconciled With the Leasehold
Mortgage as Drafted
Severity: Critical. Sources: Term Sheet Section 9 (Lender Protections) and
Section 16; Bayside Term Sheet Sections 5.3, 5.4, 5.5, 5.6, 5.7; Instructions
Section 2.
Record facts. The Term Sheet’s New Lease right arises only “[i]n the event the
Lease is terminated due to an Event of Default by Tenant,” and requires the
mortgagee to cure within 60 days (monetary) and 90 days (non-monetary)
“following the date of termination.” Bayside’s Section 5.3 requires a new lease if
the ground lease is terminated “for any reason whatsoever,” on request within
60 days after the lender’s receipt of notice of termination, with cure periods
running from the effective date of the New Lease, Landlord executing within 30
days, equal priority, and recognition of all subleases. Bayside’s Section 5.4
provides that “no termination of the Ground Lease shall be effective unless
and until Lender has received such notice and the applicable Lender cure
periods… have expired without cure,” and that the lender’s cure periods run
“in addition to, and not concurrent with” the tenant’s cure periods. The Term
Sheet says the opposite: termination is automatic on expiry of the 180-day
milestone cure. Bayside’s Section 5.5 (no amendment, modification, or termination
of the ground lease without lender consent) and Section 5.7 (bankruptcy
protections) have no counterpart in the Term Sheet at all.
Analysis. These are not stylistic differences; each is a condition precedent to loan
closing under Bayside Section 6(a) (“containing all provisions set forth in Section 5
above”). As written, the Term Sheet fails at least four of them, and the “automatic
termination” feature makes the New Lease right largely illusory: a lender cannot
elect a new lease if the lease has already terminated and the improvements have
vested in the fee owner free of its claims. The Term Sheet’s New Lease right also
does not state the priority of the new lease, does not require recognition of
subleases, and does not run the cure periods from the new lease date — all of
which Bayside requires.
Position taken. Article 10 is drafted to Bayside’s checklist rather than to the Term
Sheet: Section 10.1 (leasehold mortgage with no consent or fee), 10.2
(simultaneous notices, with an express consequence for failure), 10.3 (independent
cure periods — 60/90/180 days, additional and not concurrent), 10.4 (New Lease
on any termination, request within 60 days, execution within 30 days, effective as
of termination, equal priority, subleases recognized, improvements included, no


                                       Page 7 of 31
                      Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


additional rent), 10.5 (no surrender or amendment without consent), 10.6
(foreclosure and assignment without consent), 10.7 (SNDA with the eight required
elements), 10.8 (bankruptcy), and 10.9–10.11 (multiple mortgagees, no merger,
mortgagee not obligated). “Leasehold Mortgagee” is defined broadly through
“Qualified Mortgagee” so that the protections survive refinancing — the point you
made about a lender in 2025 and a lender in 2065.
Fallback. None of these are tradeable in substance; if the Authority resists, the
response is that these provisions are conditions to Bayside’s closing and therefore
to the deal.

Issue 4 — The Tax Abatement Is Not Yet an Asset
Severity: Critical. Sources: Term Sheet Section 10; Resolution (recital and
Section 3(d)); Instructions Section 4; Title Commitment Exception No. 7;
Bayside Term Sheet Section 6 (conditions).
Record facts. The Term Sheet structures a fifteen-year phase-in at 25%, 50%, and
75% of full post-development assessed value, is explicit that the abatement “period
shall commence on the Lease Commencement Date,” and obligates the Authority
only to “use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain all approvals and
authorizations necessary to implement the tax abatement program.” The
Resolution recites that the abatement is “to be implemented through a separate
tax abatement agreement between Meridian and the City of Port Calloway,” and
authorizes the Executive Director to execute such an agreement. No such
agreement is in the record, and no City action appears anywhere in the record.
Analysis. (a) Value. At the projected $178,000,000 assessed value and 34.75 mills,
full taxes would be $6,185,500 per year. Over fifteen years the un-abated burden
would be $92,782,500; the abated burden is $46,391,250. The abatement is
therefore worth approximately $46.4 million in nominal dollars — half the
fifteen-year burden, and the single largest economic item in the deal after rent
itself.
   (b) Timing. Because the abatement clock starts on the Lease Commencement
       Date (October 1, 2025) but rent does not commence until October 1, 2028,
       roughly three of the fifteen Abatement Years are consumed before the
       Project produces income — and during those years the property is assessed
       as unimproved land at $4,200,000, so the abatement delivers almost nothing
       ($36,488 per year at the 25% tier) while the 2038–2040 years, which would
       otherwise fall at the 75% tier, fall at 100%. Deferring the start to the Rent
       Commencement Date is worth approximately $4.6 million in nominal terms
       on unchanged assumptions (three years at $1,546,375 of forgone
       abatement). This is precisely the point in your instructions about maximizing
       the benefit during the income-producing years.
   (c) Enforceability. The Authority is a redevelopment authority; it does not levy
       or collect real property taxes. The City’s assessor, Board of Finance, and
       legislative body do. A promise by the Authority to provide an abatement is
       therefore not self-executing, and “commercially reasonable efforts” is not an
       enforceable commitment to deliver value. The statutory vehicle by which a
       Connecticut municipality may fix assessments or abate taxes for a

                                      Page 8 of 31
                       Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


      commercial project must be confirmed with municipal counsel, together with
      the permissible duration; the record identifies none, and a fifteen-year
      phase-in may exceed what the enabling authority permits. This is a diligence
      question we cannot answer from the record and should be put to Howard
      Koss in writing early.
   (d) Interaction with the tax covenant. Under a triple-net structure you covenant
       to pay all taxes. If the abatement fails, the covenant produces the opposite of
       the underwritten economics, and the Term Sheet gives you no remedy.
Position taken. Section 6.3(b) requires a recorded, City-executed, Tenant-
enforceable tax abatement agreement as a condition to the Lease Commencement
Date; Section 6.3(a) fixes the Abatement Commencement Date at the later of the
Rent Commencement Date and the first year in which the Project is assessed as
improved property; Section 6.3(c) upgrades “commercially reasonable efforts” to
diligent efforts with a representation that no undisclosed municipal action is
required; and Section 6.4 provides a dollar-for-dollar Base Rent offset for any
shortfall, plus a termination right if the shortfall persists for two consecutive
Abatement Years or exceeds $3,000,000. Section 6.2 excludes PILOT payments
and taxes on the adjoining parcel.
Fallback. If the Authority insists that the abatement begin on the Lease
Commencement Date, quantify the concession at approximately $4.6 million and
trade it for a corresponding reduction elsewhere — most efficiently, an equivalent
rent abatement in the first two Rent Years, which produces nearly the same after-
tax effect and requires no City action. If the City will not commit in a recorded
agreement before closing, require an escrow or a Base Rent offset (Section 6.4) as
the commercial substitute, and do not sign without one of the two.
Open item. Confirm (i) the statutory authority for and permissible duration of the
abatement; (ii) whether any special taxing district levy applies in addition to the
34.75 mill City rate; and (iii) that the City will be the contracting party. See Part
VIII.

Issue 5 — Percentage Rent: A Participation Mechanism That Becomes a
Gross Receipts Tax
Severity: High. Sources: Term Sheet Section 6; Instructions Section 3.
Record facts. Percentage rent is 2.0% of annual Gross Revenues above a
$28,500,000 Breakpoint, commencing in “Lease Year 6.” Term Sheet Section 6
states that the Breakpoint “is a fixed dollar amount and shall remain unchanged
for the duration of the Lease Term,” and that specific exclusions from Gross
Revenues “shall be defined in the Definitive Lease.” Gross Revenues are defined as
all rents, fees, charges, and other income of every kind generated from the
operation of the Project. Percentage rent statements are to be prepared “in
accordance with generally accepted accounting principles consistently applied,”
with books and records kept for three years and an annual Landlord audit right
with a 5% cost-shifting threshold.
Analysis. The Term Sheet’s structure produces a mismatch that grows without
limit. Base Rent escalates at a fixed 2.5%; the Breakpoint does not escalate at all.


                                       Page 9 of 31
                       Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


Percentage rent is therefore levied on an ever-larger real base, and the effective
participation rate rises every year in real terms. On an illustrative path of
$30,000,000 of Gross Revenues in Rent Year 6 growing at 3% per annum (the
record contains no revenue pro forma, so this is ours, not the parties’), percentage
rent rises from $30,000 in Year 6 to $8.8 million in Year 99, and from about 0.9%
of Base Rent to about 30% of Base Rent. Aggregated over Rent Years 6 through 99,

foreclosure bidder. Because the instrument is recorded and binds successors, a

Issue 13 — Definitional Defect: “Lease Year” Means Two Different Things
Severity: Medium (but pervasive). Sources: Term Sheet Sections 3 and 5.
Record facts. Term Sheet Section 3 measures the 99-year Term and the Lease
Expiration Date from the Lease Commencement Date, and Section 5 states that
“the first day of Lease Year 1 (which shall be the Rent Commencement Date)” is
when escalation begins. Section 6 begins percentage rent in “Lease Year 6”;
Section 10 commences the tax abatement on the Lease Commencement Date;
Section 9 measures milestones from the Lease Commencement Date.
Analysis. One defined term is used on two different clocks, three years apart. Left
uncorrected, “Lease Year 6” for percentage rent could mean October 1, 2030
(three years earlier than intended) or October 1, 2033; the tax abatement “Years 1
through 5” could mean 2025–2030 or 2028–2033; and the CPI resets could occur
three years early. Across a 99-year instrument, this single ambiguity affects every
economic term in the lease.
Position taken. The Draft Lease does not use “Lease Year.” Section 1.1 defines
“Term Year” (from the Lease Commencement Date) and “Rent Year” (from the
Rent Commencement Date), with a drafting note explaining the substitution, and
each Article specifies which applies. Base Rent escalation, CPI resets, and
percentage rent run on Rent Years; the Term, the Milestones, and the abatement
period run on Term Years or their own defined periods.
Fallback. If Landlord’s counsel will not accept the substitution, retain “Lease Year”
but define it distinctly for each Article (e.g., “Lease Year (Rent)” and “Lease Year
(Term)”). Do not leave the Term Sheet formulation in place.

VI. LEGAL RESEARCH NOTE — RELIEF FROM THE REVERTER
The question. You asked whether Connecticut law provides relief against a
forfeiture of improvements grossly disproportionate to the landlord’s harm, and
whether a principle from a prior Stamford matter applies.
Preliminary view, subject to verification. Connecticut applies the conventional
liquidated-damages-versus-penalty analysis: a stipulated remedy will be enforced
if, at the time of contracting, the harm from breach was difficult to estimate and
the stipulated amount was a reasonable forecast of that harm; if the amount is
unreasonably large in light of anticipated or actual loss, it is treated as a penalty
and is unenforceable. The candidate authority we would start with is Nor’easter
Group, Inc. v. Colossale Concrete, Inc., 207 Conn. 468 (1988); we have not yet
pulled or shepardized the opinion, and you should treat it as a research lead rather
than a conclusion. The Restatement provisions most likely to be useful are Section
356 (liquidated damages and penalties), Section 229 (excuse of a condition to
avoid disproportionate forfeiture), and Section 208 (unconscionability); each is
persuasive in Connecticut but not binding, and we would need to confirm how the
Superior Court has applied them to lease forfeitures. Restatement (Second) of
Property (Landlord and Tenant) Section 13.2 (relief from forfeiture) is a further
possible source, again as persuasion rather than authority.
On the Stamford recollection. We have not located any authority in the record,
or in preliminary research, connecting a Stamford project to a disproportionate-
forfeiture holding, and we do not recommend relying on a recollection we cannot
verify. If you can identify the transaction or the decision, we will check it. Our
working hypothesis is that the principle you recall is the penalty doctrine described
above, or the Restatement’s disproportionate-forfeiture rule, rather than a distinct
leasing decision.

                                             Page 25 of 31
                         Privileged & Confidential — Attorney Work Product — Hargrove & Linden LLP


Why the legal argument should not be the plan. Three reasons. First, a court
asked to enforce an express, negotiated reverter in a 99-year ground lease with
sophisticated parties may well enforce it: freedom of contract is a strong default,
and the forfeiture is not of a statutory or procedural right but of improvements a
party agreed to build subject to a stated risk. Second, litigation is a remedy after
the loss, and you would be litigating over an asset the Authority then holds. Third,
and dispositive commercially, the objection is not only legal but underwriting:
Bayside will not close on a lease that permits its collateral to be extinguished
without compensation, so the provision must be fixed in negotiation regardless of
how a Connecticut court might later rule (Issue 3).
What the record does support. The strongest negotiating materials are factual
rather than legal: (a) the deposit already secures performance, so the Authority is
not without a remedy; (b) the Authority’s actual damages from a delay are
quantifiable and modest — no Base Rent is payable before the Rent
Commencement Date, so a missed Milestone 3 costs months of ground rent, not
hundreds of millions; (c) the ratio between the forfeiture (roughly $211–264 million
of Phase 1 improvements, plus $5.7 million) and the harm is on the order of a
thousand to one; and (d) the Authority’s own Resolution requires reverter rights
but does not require that they be uncompensated.
Two further points to verify with Connecticut counsel. First, whether a
redevelopment authority leasing public land for 99 years may enforce a forfeiture
of a lessee’s improvements, and whether any statutory or public-purpose limitation
constrains it. Second, whether the enabling legislation for a disposition of this
duration requires municipal legislative approval beyond the Authority’s own board
action; the record contains the Board’s resolution but no City Council action, and
the Resolution relies on counsel’s advice rather than identifying the statutory grant
(Issue 11 of the log).

IX. ASSUMPTIONS AND LIMITATIONS
  1. Source of factual statements. All factual statements about the Premises,
     the parties, and the economics are taken from the seven documents listed in
     Part I. We have not conducted fieldwork, title examination, or an
     independent environmental investigation, and we have not verified the
     Survey in the field.
  2. The Survey is assumed correct. Issue 1 is resolved in the Draft Lease in
     favor of the Survey description because it is internally consistent (the
     courses close, and the frontages match the narrative), because it is certified
     to the parties and the lender under the ALTA/NSPS standard, and because
     the Title Commitment’s Exhibit A computes to roughly double the stated
     area. That conclusion should be confirmed by the surveyor before recording.
  3. Revenue illustrations are ours. The record contains no revenue pro
     forma. The percentage rent illustrations in Issue 5 and Exhibit B assume
     $30,000,000 of Gross Revenues in Rent Year 6 growing at 3% per annum,
     solely to demonstrate the mechanics of a fixed versus an indexed
     Breakpoint. The actual projection may be materially different, and the
     illustration should be replaced with your own model before it is shown to
     anyone outside this firm.
  4. Tax computations hold value and rate constant. The abatement figures
     assume the $178,000,000 assessed value and 34.75 mill rate stated in the
     Term Sheet, applied uniformly across the Term, without revaluation,
     inflation, discounting, or phase-in of the improvements’ assessment. They
     are illustrative, as the Term Sheet itself states.
  5. Phase 1 cost allocation is an inference. The $211–264 million estimate of
     Phase 1 improvements at risk assumes the residential tower and podium
     represent 60–75% of the $352,000,000 hard cost budget. The record does
     not allocate hard costs between phases.
  6. Legal research is preliminary. Part VI is a research summary, not an
     opinion. The Connecticut authorities cited should be pulled and verified, and
     we express no view on whether the reverter would be enforced.
  7. Environmental conclusions are the consultant’s. The contamination
     descriptions, the $2,400,000 estimate, and the ELUR interpretation are
     Dr. Okafor’s; our contribution is the arithmetic check in Part V and the
     allocation analysis.
  8. Documents not reviewed. The loan documents, the Coventry preferred
     equity documents, the construction contracts, the general contractor’s GMP,
     the Authority’s bylaws, and its waterfront revitalization plan as readopted in
     2022 were not provided.
  9. No opinion on statutory authority. We express no view on whether the
     Authority’s enabling legislation permits a 99-year lease at this rent, or
     whether municipal legislative approval is required beyond the Board’s
     resolution (log Issue 11). That question should be put to Howard Koss
     directly.
  10.Rounding. Percentage and per-square-foot figures are rounded for
     presentation; the operative documents govern.
