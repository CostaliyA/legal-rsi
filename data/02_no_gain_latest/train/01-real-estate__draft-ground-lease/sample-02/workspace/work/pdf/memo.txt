5.1 What the record actually provides
Term Sheet § 9 provides that if Tenant fails to achieve Milestone 1 or Milestone 3 (as
extended), the Authority may deliver a notice specifying the failure; Tenant then has
180 days to cure; and "the Lease shall automatically terminate upon the expiration of
such period," whereupon (a) Tenant forfeits the $5,700,000 letter of credit, which the
Authority "shall be entitled to draw in full," and (b) "[a]ll improvements, structures,
and fixtures constructed, installed, or placed on the Premises by Tenant ... shall
immediately revert to and become the sole property of the Landlord, free and clear of
any claims by Tenant, without any payment, compensation, or reimbursement to
Tenant."
Four features of that drafting deserve to be named, because each is a separate
problem:
     •     It is automatic. Termination occurs by operation of the expiry of the cure
           period, without any election, notice or grace. That is the feature that collides
           head-on with Bayside § 5.4 (conflict C11).
     •     It is not limited to milestones. Term Sheet § 16 lists "exercise the reverter
           rights" as a general remedy for any uncured Event of Default, which would
           extend forfeiture to a monetary default, a bankruptcy filing or an insurance
           lapse (conflict C12).
     •     It double-counts. The Authority takes both the deposit and the
           improvements. The deposit is already 24 months of Base Rent; the
           improvements may be worth fifty times that.
     •     It purports to extinguish the mortgage. "Free and clear of any claims by
           Tenant" cannot, as a matter of recorded priority, extinguish a leasehold
           mortgagee's interest, and Bayside will not treat it as doing so. But it is exactly
           the language that makes the leasehold mortgage unmarketable, because the
           lender's only remedy — foreclosure into the leasehold — leads to the forfeiture
           it was lending against.
The Board has been told that the lease will contain "reverter rights in favor of the
Authority in the event of Tenant's failure to achieve such milestones" (Resolution No.
2025-047, § 2(a)). The Draft Lease preserves a reverter. What it removes is the
uncompensated forfeiture. That distinction is the single most important framing point
in the negotiation, and it is accurate: the Authority still gets the land back, still gets
the improvements, and still gets a defined remedy.

5.3 Is it enforceable? Our assessment, and its limits
You asked us to research whether Connecticut law provides relief against a
disproportionate forfeiture, and you recalled a "Stamford project" case. We have not
verified a Connecticut case on point, and we should not cite one until we have.
What follows is our assessment of the governing principles and of the risks on both
sides; Part 7 identifies the research that must be completed before any of this is put in
front of Mr. Koss or the Board.
The argument for Tenant
The provision has two doctrinal vulnerabilities. First, penalty. A contractual term that
fixes damages in an amount grossly disproportionate to the loss that the parties could
reasonably have anticipated is an unenforceable penalty rather than a valid liquidated
damages provision. The Restatement (Second) of Contracts states the rule at § 356(1),
and Connecticut follows the general rule. On the numbers in Part 5.2 above, the
disproportion is on the order of 28× to 54×, which is well outside any range a court is
likely to accept as a forecast of harm.
Second, forfeiture. Equity relieves against forfeiture where the forfeiture would be
disproportionate to the breach and the injured party can be adequately compensated
(Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 208, 229; the Restatement (Third) of Property
(Landlord and Tenant) treats forfeiture as a disfavoured remedy). A 99-year ground
lease under which a tenant invests $425 million on land it does not own is the
paradigm case for the application of that principle, and the Authority's own internal
documents describe the public benefits it expects to receive from the completed
project.
Third, and practically most important: the leasehold mortgagee's interest is a
separate property interest. A provision that purports to wipe it out "free and clear of
any claims" will be tested against a lender with $285 million at stake and its own
counsel. Whatever the outcome, the Authority would be litigating against a secured
creditor with priority, not merely against Meridian.
The argument against Tenant — and why we must not rely on the defense
There are two credible counter-arguments, and you should hear them clearly. (i)
Forfeiture of a leasehold is different. Courts have traditionally enforced conditional
limitations and forfeiture clauses in leases more readily than they strike liquidated
damages clauses in ordinary commercial contracts, on the theory that the landlord's
reversion is a property right and the tenant takes subject to the condition. (ii)
Sophisticated parties, public landlord. The Authority will argue that Meridian is a
sophisticated developer that negotiated a public-benefit-laden ground lease with
counsel, that the milestones are public obligations, and that a court should not rewrite
the bargain that a public body obtained for public land.
So the provision might well be enforced. That is precisely why an unenforceability
argument is not a strategy: Bayside will not lend $285 million against a litigable
defense, and by the time the question is answered, the project will have failed. The
provision must be fixed in the document. If the Authority's counsel argues that the

5.4 What the Draft Lease does
The reverter is a defined, sequenced, compensated remedy. Mechanically, the Draft
Lease:
    •   Retains the reverter for Milestone 1 and Milestone 3 only, and expressly
        confines it there: failure of Milestone 2 or Milestone 4 gives no remedy beyond
        notice and consultation (§ 3.5), and the Milestone remedy is Landlord's "sole
        and exclusive remedy" for a milestone default (§ 10.3).
    •   Removes automatic termination (§ 10.5(f)), substituting a valid notice, a
        180-day Tenant cure period, a 180-day lender cure period, and a 30-day
        election window — after which the Authority is deemed to have waived that
        milestone default (§ 10.5(e)). Waiver matters: it prevents an Authority from
        sitting on a stale default for years and then terminating.
    •   Requires the Improvement Payment (§ 10.6(a)): the greater of the
        appraised fair market value of the Improvements or Tenant's unreimbursed
        hard costs, less insurance and condemnation recoveries not applied to
        restoration, less Landlord's Documented Damages. That is your option (a) and
        your option (b) from the June 15 email, in the alternative rather than the
        conjunctive, with the higher of the two governing.
    •   Caps the Authority's damages (§ 10.7) at accrued rent, 24 months of Base
        Rent at the then-current rate, re-letting costs and enforcement costs, and
        excludes lost Percentage Rent, lost tax revenue, the value of the
        Improvements, and consequential and speculative amounts.
    •   Follows the compensation with real teeth (§ 10.6(c)): the Improvement
        Payment is due within 60 days, may be secured by a first lien on the
        Improvements at Tenant's election, and the Authority may not take possession
        or lease the Improvements to a third party until it has paid or secured.
    •   Makes the reverter subordinate to the leasehold mortgage and conditional
        on the lender's rights being exhausted (§ 10.5(g)), which is the Term Sheet
        change Sandra Milliken requires.
    •   Keeps the Authority whole on the public side (§ 10.6(f)): on any reverter
        the Authority takes subject to the ELUR, the affordable housing covenant, all
        subleases and the open-space obligation — which is what the Authority
        actually wants, since it will be the owner of an operating building.
    •   Adds a paid escape valve (§ 3.4(d), bracketed): Tenant may buy 6-month
        extensions of Milestone 1 or 3 rather than face forfeiture. For a landlord, an
        extension fee is a certain receipt; for a tenant, it is a way to survive a
        permitting overrun. It is often the provision that lets both sides sign.

5.5 How to sell it to the Authority
The Authority's counsel will treat the reverter as a deal point it cannot give up,
because the Board resolution recites it. The argument that has the best chance of
moving them is not proportionality rhetoric; it is the following, in this order:
    •   An unenforceable remedy is worth nothing. If a court strikes the forfeiture
        as a penalty, or relieves against the forfeiture, the Authority is left with a lease
        still in force, a tenant it wants to remove, and no remedy at all. A compensated
        reverter is a remedy a court will enforce.
    •   The lender is the reason. The Authority's own recital records that the
        ground lease must contain provisions satisfactory to the construction lender
        (Resolution recital). Bayside has said in terms that the reverter as written
        would jeopardize the leasehold mortgage. The Authority cannot have both an
        uncompensated reverter and a $285 million construction loan.
    •   The Authority still gets the building. On a reverter at 95% complete the
        Authority acquires a nearly finished residential tower and retail podium,
        subject to a defined payment that is largely funded by the value of the asset it
        is receiving. It is not paying $300 million; it is paying the appraised value out
        of the asset it keeps.
    •   The Authority's real leverage is elsewhere, and we will give it that
        leverage. If the Authority wants enforcement teeth, we will trade: extension
        fees and delay charges payable to the Authority; a step-up in Base Rent during
        any period of milestone delay; Board reporting on construction progress; a
        right to approve a replacement developer; and the 180-day cure running from
        the Authority's notice rather than from any later event. Those are teeth that
        bite without destroying the collateral.
Walk-away position. If the Authority will not move on compensation, the minimum
acceptable outcome is: (i) no forfeiture of the Improvements where Phase 1 is more
than 50% complete; (ii) no forfeiture for delay caused by permitting, DEEP or coastal
approvals, or by the Authority's own acts; (iii) no forfeiture while a lender is curing;
(iv) the deposit applied to actual damages with the excess returned; and (v) the
Improvement Payment limited to hard cost reimbursement net of recoveries and
damages. If none of that is available, the transaction should not proceed, because the
loan will not close.

6.3 ELUR: the ground-floor residential prohibition against a ground-floor
residential program
The ELUR prohibits residential use on the ground floor of any structure on the
Premises because near-surface soils exceed the Residential Direct Exposure Criteria.
The Term Sheet describes the Project as including "ground-floor residential lobby and
amenity spaces (fitness center, package room, resident lounge, and leasing office)" and
permits "residential apartments" without a floor restriction. Halcyon addressed the
tension directly and did not resolve it: the ELUR defines residential use as "habitable
space at or below finished grade elevation intended for residential dwelling, sleeping,
cooking, or eating purposes" and "does not define or specifically address non-habitable
spaces such as building lobbies, mail rooms, fitness centers, package rooms, leasing
offices, or common amenity areas." Halcyon's recommendation is a formal DEEP
interpretation or variance before the design is finalized.
This matters in three ways that are easy to miss. First, permitting. DEEP approval is
on the critical path to Milestone 1, and an ELUR enforcement question can stop a
building permit. Second, the milestone clock. If DEEP's interpretation requires a
redesign of the ground floor, the delay will count against Milestone 1 (18 months)
unless it is expressly treated as a force majeure event or a permitting delay — which is
why Section 7.8(b) and Section 3.4 say so. Third, cost allocation. An ELUR
modification application is expensive, and it is expensive because of a pre-existing
condition the Authority created and disclosed in 2018, not because of anything Tenant
did. Section 7.8(a) puts that cost on the Authority.
There is a helpful physical fact in the record that the parties should not lose: the
project's ground floor will be at finished floor elevation 15 feet NAVD88, while
existing site grades run from approximately 10.2 to 14.8 feet NAVD88 and the BFE is
12 feet. On the ELUR's own definition (space "at or below finished grade elevation"), a
slab at 15.0 feet is above grade across essentially the whole site. That is a strong
argument that the ground floor is not within the restricted zone at all — but it is an
argument, not a determination, and DEEP has not been asked. Section 7.8(c) records
the point for use in the application.
Two related ELUR obligations that appear nowhere in the Term Sheet and are now in
the Draft Lease: the ELUR's prohibition on potable water supply wells (Section 7.7(e)),
and the annual ELUR compliance certification to DEEP (Section 7.7(f)). The latter
raises an allocation question the Term Sheet does not answer: the certification is
required of the "owner, operator, or designee," and the fee owner is the Authority.
Section 7.7 obliges the Authority to join in any filing requiring the fee owner's
signature within ten business days. Finally, note the interaction between the 24-inch
engineered barrier and the 8,000 square feet of public open space: landscaping and
tree planting over a capped area is constrained by both the barrier specification and
the prohibition on ground disturbance below two feet without a DEEP-approved Soil
Management Plan (Section 14.4, final sentence).

PART 10 — NEGOTIATION STRATEGY

10.1 Must-haves (walk-away if refused)
     •    Compensation for improvements on any reverter (§ 10.6) — your stated
          non-negotiable, and the condition to the loan.
     •    Lender cure periods and New Lease (§§ 9.5, 9.6) — without these the loan
          does not close, whatever the rest of the document says.
     •    A conformed legal description and a discharge of the mechanics' lien
          (§§ 2.3, 16.2) — title cannot issue otherwise.
     •    An amendment of the recorded ROFR to carve out mortgagee enforcement
          (§ 11.5) — a recorded instrument cannot be overridden by contract.
     •    A tax abatement backstop (§ 6.8) — the Authority is not the taxing authority,
          so a covenant alone is not enough.

10.2 Strong preferences (trade only against something real)
     •    Breakpoint indexation (§ 5.3) — trade against a higher Percentage Rent rate if
          pressed, or accept CPI-only indexation as a fallback.
     •    Abatement commencement re-anchoring (§ 6.7) — this is the highest-value
          item that the Authority will frame as impossible; it may need City cooperation
          and therefore time.
     •    Environmental overrun allocation and indemnity (§§ 7.4(c), 7.5) — a 50/50
          sharing above $2.4 million is an acceptable landing zone.
     •    Bulkhead allocation (§§ 2.9(b), 14.3) — get the assessment first; the number
          will do the arguing.
     •    Extension option (§ 2.7) — valuable but not worth the transaction; drop it if the
          Board resists.

10.3 Tradeables (give these if needed)
The recomputed rent amounts (Part 2.1 items 3–4 — a $54 per annum point); the CPI
base-month correction (item 16); the setoff mechanics; the flood insurance
enhancement (we would not concede this, since it protects Bayside, but we can let it
be framed as a lender requirement rather than a Tenant ask); the condominium
conversion right; and the paid-extension mechanic, which is a concession to Tenant
that costs the Authority nothing if Tenant never uses it and pays the Authority if it
does.

10.4 Sequencing
Send the draft and this schedule to Mr. Koss with the loan-driven items identified as
lender requirements, not Tenant preferences. That framing is accurate — Bayside § 5
requires most of them — and it moves the argument from "the tenant wants more" to
"the deal does not fund without this." Then schedule a joint call with Authority counsel
and Bayside's counsel in the same week, so that the Authority hears the lender's
requirements from the lender. We should not negotiate the reverter with Authority
counsel alone and then have to re-open it when Bayside's redline arrives.
