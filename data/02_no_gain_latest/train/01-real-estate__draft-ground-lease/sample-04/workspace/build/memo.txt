@H1 III. THE SIX CRITICAL ISSUES
@H2 A. The Reverter Right (Item 1)
@H3 1. What the Term Sheet does
@P Section 9 provides that if Tenant fails to achieve Milestone 1 (commencement of construction by April 1, 2027) or Milestone 3 (substantial completion of Phase 1 by October 1, 2030), the Authority may deliver a notice specifying the failure; Tenant then has 180 days to cure; and if it does not, "the Lease shall automatically terminate upon the expiration of such period," whereupon (a) Tenant forfeits the $5,700,000 letter of credit, which the Authority "shall be entitled to draw in full," and (b) "[a]ll improvements, structures, and fixtures constructed, installed, or placed on the Premises by Tenant... shall immediately revert to and become the sole property of the Landlord, free and clear of any claims by Tenant, without any payment, compensation, or reimbursement to Tenant."
@H3 2. Why it fails as a matter of economics
@P The disparity is not merely large; it is inverted, and that is the point we would press with the Authority. Total hard construction cost is $352,000,000 (Loan Term Sheet Section 3). At Milestone 1, in April 2027, the Improvements will consist of footings and little else, so the forfeiture has almost no value and the Authority's injury — a year-and-a-half delay in the entire project — is at its greatest. At Milestone 3, in October 2030, the position reverses. If the milestone is missed at 90% completion, the value transferred to the Authority is on the order of $317,000,000. But by then Base Rent has been running for two years (the Rent Commencement Date is October 1, 2028), Percentage Rent does not begin until Rent Year 6 (October 2033) in any event, and the Improvements are on the tax roll subject only to the abatement phase-in. The Authority's monetary loss from a further delay is therefore at or near its lowest precisely at the moment when the forfeiture would be at its highest.
@P Taking the Authority's claim at its highest — a full year of Base Rent forgone, $2,850,000 — the ratio at Milestone 3 is roughly 111:1. Add the $5,700,000 letter of credit, which is forfeited in addition, and the ratio exceeds 113:1. If the Authority's loss is measured correctly (the delay in tax receipts as the Improvements come onto the roll, and the deferral of the residual value of the Improvements at the end of a ninety-nine-year Term), the ratio is larger still. A remedy that is at its most severe where the injury is at its least is the definition of a penalty.
@P The comparison to the Authority's actual damages is not merely an argument we would make after the fact. It is the test a court would apply, and it is the test that matters for negotiation, because a lender will not underwrite a collateral package whose value turns on a clause that a court might or might not reform after eighteen months of litigation.
@H3 3. What the Term Sheet already gives away
@P Two features of Section 9 make the position worse than it appears on first reading. First, the reverter is triggered by a delay "even if Meridian is 95% complete," as you put it, because there is no completion threshold, no materiality qualifier, and no requirement that Tenant have abandoned the project. Second, the trigger is objective: a missed date, not a failure to perform. A permitting holdup at the Building Department, a supply-chain interruption in curtain wall, or a DEEP objection to the ELUR would all produce a missed date without any fault on Meridian's part.
@H3 4. Our position
@P We have drafted Article 9 to preserve a reverter in form, because the Authority's Board has resolved that the Lease include "reverter rights in favor of the Authority" (Resolution No. 2025-047, Section 2(a)) and because we do not think the Authority will abandon the concept at the first draft. But we have surrounded it with six conditions precedent, each of which independently defeats it: (a) the improvements cannot vest in the Authority unless and until the Authority pays a Purchase Price; (b) the Purchase Price is the greater of the appraised Fair Market Value of the Improvements and Tenant's Unreimbursed Hard Costs, net only of insurance, Landlord's documented damages, and third-party recoveries; (c) Tenant may elect the Fair Market Value measure and extinguish any Landlord damages claim; (d) if the Authority does not pay, the termination is void, the Lease continues, and the Milestone is deemed satisfied; (e) there is no forfeiture of the security deposit, which is limited to actual documented loss; and (f) the reverter is unavailable altogether if the work is 90% or more complete (measured by hard costs), in which case the Authority's remedies are specific performance and damages only.
@P Separately, we have deleted the "automatic termination" language. Automatic termination is not merely harsh; it is structurally incompatible with the lender protections the same section purports to grant, because a lease that terminates by operation of its own terms cannot be cured, and because the New Lease right depends on there being an identifiable termination event and a notice to the Lender.
@H3 5. Conditions to the reverter that are not currently in the Term Sheet
@P The draft adds four conditions that the Term Sheet omits and that we regard as non-negotiable: no reverter if the failure is attributable to Force Majeure, to Landlord or City delay, to any unmet condition precedent, or to a DEEP review of the ELUR; no reverter unless Tenant has ceased work for 180 consecutive days; no reverter until every Leasehold Mortgagee has exhausted its cure rights and declined a New Lease; and no reverter without a compliant Reverter Notice specifying the Milestone Date as extended. Each of those is drafted as a condition precedent rather than a covenant, because a covenant gives rise to damages while a condition defeats the power.

@H2 B. Lender Protections and the New Lease Right (Item 2)
@P The gap between what the Term Sheet grants and what Bayside requires is wider than it first appears. The Term Sheet's lender protections (Section 9) are a fair summary, but Bayside's Loan Term Sheet Section 5 is a checklist, every element of which is a condition precedent to closing (Section 6(a)). The differences are material:

@P The most consequential omission is the independent lender cure period for milestone defaults. A milestone failure is a non-monetary default, so under Bayside's Section 5.4 the Lender gets 90 days from its own receipt of notice, running after Tenant's period — a total of roughly 270 days before the Authority can act. Your instruction asks for at least 180 days from the Lender's receipt of notice. We have drafted the longer of the two (180 days in Section 10.3(c)), which satisfies Bayside's floor and your instruction, and we have added the Lender's express right to install a replacement developer, general contractor, or property manager, which is the mechanism by which a lender actually cures a construction milestone.
@P Two related points. First, the Term Sheet gives the Leasehold Mortgagee a New Lease right only on a Tenant default; Bayside requires it on any termination, including the reverter. We have drafted the broader right (Section 10.5) and, in Section 9.9, made the reverter expressly subordinate to it. Second, the Term Sheet provides no mechanism for the Landlord to deliver an SNDA, estoppel, or recognition agreement on a refinancing. Over ninety-nine years you will refinance repeatedly, and each refinancing will require the Authority's cooperation. Section 10.9 imposes a ten-business-day deadline with a self-help remedy and permits the Tenant to deliver the instrument on the Landlord's behalf on certification of failure.

@H2 C. The Tax Abatement: Timing, and Who Is Bound (Item 3)
@H3 1. The timing problem
@P Term Sheet Section 10 provides that the abatement period "shall commence on the Lease Commencement Date." The Lease Commencement Date is October 1, 2025. The Rent Commencement Date is thirty-six months later (October 1, 2028) or earlier on a certificate of occupancy. The consequence is that three of the fifteen Abatement Years are consumed before the Project produces a dollar of revenue.
@P There is a second and worse consequence, which we do not believe was intended. The Abatement Years 1–5 phase is defined as twenty-five percent of the **full post-development assessed value** — that is, twenty-five percent of $178,000,000, or $44,500,000 of assessed value, producing $1,546,375 of tax in each of the first five years. But during construction the Premises are assessed as land only at $4,200,000, producing $145,950. Applied literally, the "abatement" would **increase** your tax bill during construction from approximately $145,950 to $1,546,375 — an increase of $1,400,425 per year, or $4,201,275 over the three-year construction period. An abatement should not operate as a surcharge.
@P The aggregate effect is material. Computed on the Term Sheet structure, taxes from 2025 through 2043 total approximately $71.1 million in nominal terms. Computed with the abatement period beginning on the Rent Commencement Date, and with Construction Period taxes based on the actual land assessment, the same period totals approximately $53.0 million — a difference of approximately $18.1 million, and a delay of the first full-assessment year from 2040 to 2043. The tables in Section IV set out the computation.
@H3 2. The authority problem — this is the more serious of the two
@P The Abatement Program is not something the Authority can grant. It is an assessment phase-in that must be implemented by the City of Port Calloway, through the Board of Finance and possibly the Office of Policy and Management (Term Sheet Section 10 correctly refers to "any required action by the Port Calloway Board of Finance or the Connecticut Office of Policy and Management"). The Authority's own Resolution No. 2025-047 confirms this: it "supports" a fifteen-year abatement "to be implemented through a separate tax abatement agreement between Meridian and the City of Port Calloway" (Recital) and authorizes the Executive Director to execute "any tax abatement agreement with the City of Port Calloway" (Section 3(d)). The Authority is not the City. It cannot bind the City, and the City is not a party to either the Term Sheet or the ground lease.
@P Meanwhile the lease is triple net: "Tenant shall be solely responsible for the payment of all real property taxes... and other impositions of every kind." So the ground lease gives you an unqualified obligation to pay whatever the City assesses, and no enforceable right to the abatement that makes the pro forma work. Coventry's underwriting assumes the full fifteen-year abatement during the income-producing years (your instruction, Item 4). As drafted, that assumption is unsupported by any binding instrument in the record.
@H3 3. Our position
@P We have made the abatement three things in the draft. First, an express condition precedent to the Commencement Date: the City must have executed a binding abatement agreement, with evidence of the required approvals (Section 2.7(e)). Second, a covenant of the Landlord to use its best efforts to cause the City to adopt and maintain the program (Section 6.2(c)). Third, a self-executing economic remedy: if the program is not implemented or lapses, Tenant may offset against Base Rent the excess taxes it actually pays, with interest, and the Landlord must confirm to the Lender that the offset is permitted (Section 6.2(d)). We have also moved the abatement period to the Rent Commencement Date, based Construction Period taxes on the actual land assessment, and added a re-opener if the post-development assessed value exceeds the projection by more than 20% (Section 6.6).
@P Practically: the abatement agreement with the City needs to be on the closing checklist now, not in the lease negotiation. We recommend a separate workstream with the City's corporation counsel, running in parallel with the lease.

@H2 D. Percentage Rent Over Ninety-Nine Years (Item 4)
@P The mechanism is 2.0% of Gross Revenues above a $28,500,000 annual breakpoint, beginning in Lease Year 6. Term Sheet Section 6 states that the breakpoint "is a fixed dollar amount and shall remain unchanged for the duration of the Lease Term." Over a term of ninety-nine years that is not a participation; it is a permanent share of gross receipts that grows without limit as a share of revenue.
@P The arithmetic is stark. Assume stabilized revenue of $30,000,000 in Rent Year 6 and revenue growth of 3% per annum. On a fixed breakpoint, percentage rent rises from $30,000 in Rent Year 6 to $236,350 in Rent Year 16, $886,357 in Rent Year 36, $2,964,962 in Rent Year 66, and $8,010,280 in Rent Year 96. If the breakpoint instead grew at the same 3%, percentage rent would remain constant in real terms at approximately $30,000. The difference over the Term is on the order of $60 million in nominal dollars, and it arises entirely from the failure to index a number that was set by reference to 2025 rents and 2025 occupancy.
@P Our primary position (Section 5.3) is that the Breakpoint for each Rent Year equals $28,500,000 multiplied by Base Rent for that Rent Year divided by Initial Base Rent. That preserves the 10:1 relationship between the Breakpoint and Base Rent that the parties struck, keeps the mechanism a participation rather than a tax, and is administratively trivial. Our fallback positions, in order of preference: (i) index the Breakpoint by CPI from the Rent Commencement Date, with a floor of $28,500,000; (ii) index by 50% of CPI; (iii) reset the Breakpoint to the greater of the fixed number and 85% of stabilized revenue at each tenth Rent Year; and (iv) in exchange for a fixed Breakpoint, cap aggregate Percentage Rent at $2,000,000 per Rent Year. We would not accept the fixed Breakpoint without at least one of these.
@P We have also tightened Gross Revenues (Section 5.2) in the manner you asked. The Term Sheet's list of exclusions is "such as security deposits not applied to rent, proceeds of insurance claims, condemnation awards, and similar items" — an open-ended illustration with no guidance. Our definition contains fifteen specific exclusions, including utility reimbursements, common area maintenance recoveries, real estate tax and insurance pass-throughs, capital contributions, financing proceeds, interest income, retroactive adjustments, contractor recoveries, bad debt, and amounts attributable to periods before the Rent Commencement Date. It also requires the reduction of Gross Revenues for rent concessions, so that free rent cannot be counted as revenue in one year and as a concession in another.

@H2 E. The Legal Description and Survey (Items 5, 12)
@P This is the item most likely to be missed and most likely to cause a closing failure, because it does not look like a problem. The Title Commitment's Exhibit A recites a metes-and-bounds description and states "[c]ontaining 3.2 acres (139,392 square feet), more or less." We ran the traverse. It closes (to within approximately 1.1 feet) and it computes to **279,535 square feet, or 6.417 acres** — approximately 2.006 times the recited area.
@P The dimensions tell the same story on their face: the Title Commitment describes 580.00 feet along Pier Street and 480.00 feet along the waterfront, which is a parcel of roughly 278,400 square feet. The surveyor, by contrast, measured 435.22 feet along Pier Street and 320.47 feet along the waterfront, which is 139,475 square feet. The Title Commitment's description is a different, larger parcel. It also bears a different set of bearings (North 72°15'30" East on the street line, as against the surveyor's South 78°15'30" East) and cites a different surveyor: "J. Michael Ferreira, P.L.S., Connecticut License No. 12847... Drawing No. CLS-2025-0321, dated April 20, 2025, and last revised May 15, 2025." The survey in the record is by Thomas R. Pellegrino, PLS-008741, Survey Job No. CLS-2025-0417, plat dated April 28, 2025. The Title Commitment is also inconsistent with itself: it cites the 2012 condemnation as recorded at Volume 367, Page 44, while the surveyor cites Volume 375, Page 44.
@P We have used the surveyor's description as Exhibit A to the Lease, verified that it closes exactly (closure error 0.00 feet), and specified in Section 2.1 that it supersedes the Title Commitment's Exhibit A. We have also made Base Rent a flat dollar amount rather than a per-square-foot amount, so that the 82.95 square foot (0.06%) difference between the surveyor's computed area (139,474.95) and the Assessor's figure (139,392) cannot be used to re-open rent. If the parties ever "corrected" the legal description to the Title Commitment's version, the per-square-foot rent would drop from $20.45 to $10.19, which is presumably not what either side intends.
@P There is a related inconsistency that we should resolve before the survey is re-certified. Every document in the record describes "approximately 480 linear feet of bulkhead" along the eastern boundary. The surveyor's own east line is 320.47 feet. The surveyor also states that the pedestrian easement area is "approximately 7,200 square feet (15 feet in width × approximately 480 feet of waterfront frontage)," but 15 feet times 320.47 feet is 4,807 square feet. The 480-foot figure appears to have been carried forward from the Title Commitment's erroneous description. This matters for three things: the scope and cost of bulkhead rehabilitation, the area of the public pedestrian easement, and the calculation of the 8,000 square foot Public Open Space requirement. Either the east line is 480 feet or the bulkhead is 320 feet; both cannot be true, and the surveyor and the Authority's own field records should be reconciled before the survey is certified to the Lender.

@H2 F. Remediation Allocation and the ELUR (Items 6, 7, 8)
@H3 1. The credit is capped; the exposure is not
@P Term Sheet Section 7 grants a fixed $1,800,000 credit against Base Rent and then makes Tenant responsible for "any actual remediation costs in excess of the $2,400,000 estimate." Read with the Phase II cost estimate, the parties' evident intent is a 75/25 split: total estimated cost $2,400,000, of which the Authority funds $1,800,000 (75%) and Meridian funds $600,000 (25%). Your instruction describes it the same way: "Meridian's share of the total $2.4 million remediation cost is $600,000 (the $1.8M credit offsets the balance)."
@P But a fixed dollar credit does not implement a percentage split. It caps the Authority's contribution and leaves 100% of the overrun with you. The Phase II report is explicit that costs "could increase materially if contamination is found to extend beyond the delineated areas during excavation, if unanticipated contaminants are encountered, or if groundwater dewatering and on-site treatment are required." Every one of those contingencies is a real risk on a former shipyard with a filled dry dock and two removed underground storage tanks whose closure documentation is "closure pending/incomplete" in the DEEP LUST database.
@P Worse, the $2,400,000 estimate excludes, by its own terms, "the cost of maintaining or modifying the existing ELUR, obtaining any necessary CT DEEP approvals or permits, preparing the required Remedial Action Plan and post-remediation Verification Report, or any legal or consulting fees associated with ELUR compliance," and it excludes bulkhead repair entirely. Those excluded categories are not incidental: they are the categories most likely to arise, and the ELUR modification is likely to be required given the ground-floor use issue discussed below.
@P We have drafted Section 8.3 to implement the 75/25 split across the whole range of Remediation Costs ($600,000 cap for Tenant up to the estimate; 75/25 sharing between 100% and 150% of the estimate; 100% to Landlord above that), and Section 8.3(d) to place 100% of off-site migration, undisclosed pre-existing conditions, groundwater contamination, and pre-Commencement regulatory directives with the Authority. We have also made the Remediation Credit assignable, non-refundable, available to a Leasehold Mortgagee and to a New Lease tenant, deferrable at Tenant's election, and payable in cash if unapplied (Section 8.4).
@H3 2. The ELUR and the ground floor
@P The ELUR prohibits "residential use on the ground floor of any structure erected on the land." The Phase II report then observes that the ELUR defines "residential use" as "habitable space at or below finished grade elevation intended for residential dwelling, sleeping, cooking, or eating purposes," and that it "does not... expressly define whether ancillary residential spaces — such as building lobbies, resident amenity rooms, fitness centers, package rooms, and leasing offices that serve a residential tower but do not themselves contain dwelling units — constitute 'residential use.'"

@H1 VI. CONNECTICUT LAW: FORFEITURE, PENALTIES, AND REMEDIES
@NOTE The following is a doctrinal summary prepared for negotiation purposes. It is not a legal opinion. The specific authorities should be confirmed by citator, and by a Connecticut litigation partner, before any of it is used in correspondence, a demand, or a brief. See Section 2.3.
@H2 6.1 The two doctrines that bear on the reverter
@P A reverter that transfers $300 million of improvements to the Authority on a missed date is exposed on two independent lines of attack, and a third that is weaker but worth preserving.
@C1 (a) **Penalty.** A contractual provision that fixes the consequences of a breach is enforceable as liquidated damages only if the harm is incapable or difficult of accurate estimation at the time of contracting and the stipulated amount is a reasonable forecast of that harm rather than a punishment. See Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356(1). If the stipulated consequence is grossly disproportionate to the actual or anticipated loss, it is a penalty and is unenforceable. Connecticut courts have long applied this framework, and the difference between the amount at stake ($317 million at 90% completion, plus $5.7 million of posted security) and the Authority's actual loss from a delay (at most approximately $2.85 million per year of Base Rent, and materially less than that at Milestone 3, when Base Rent is already being paid) is the kind of disparity that has supported penalty findings.
@C1 (b) **Forfeiture.** Separately, a court may excuse the non-occurrence of a condition — including a condition subsequent such as a reverter — where its enforcement would cause a disproportionate forfeiture, unless the condition was a material part of the agreed exchange. See Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 227(1), 229. The rule of interpretation in § 227(1) counsels in favor of an interpretation that avoids forfeiture where the language is fairly susceptible of it. This doctrine is the more powerful of the two here, because it does not require us to characterize the reverter as a damages clause; it operates on the condition itself.
@C1 (c) **Unjust enrichment and constitutional limits.** A public body that retains $300 million of privately financed improvements for a delay of months has been enriched without a corresponding legal basis, which is the classic predicate for restitution. Where the enriched party is a public body acting under color of a lease, there is also a colorable argument under Article First, § 11 of the Connecticut Constitution (private property shall not be taken for public use without just compensation) and Article First, § 1 (due process). We regard these as supplementary arguments, not the primary line: they are slower, less certain, and — critically — they are available only after the loss has occurred.
@H2 6.2 What the client asked us to confirm
@P You asked us to confirm whether Connecticut law provides relief against a disproportionate forfeiture, and referred to a prior matter "I think it was the Stamford project" involving a principle "disfavoring penalties that are grossly disproportionate to the actual harm suffered." We can confirm the principle: it is the mainstream American rule, Connecticut applies it, and it is reflected in the Restatement sections cited above. We cannot confirm the specific recollection. There is nothing in the record that identifies a Stamford decision, and we are not able to verify a citation to one from the documents in the file. We recommend a focused case-law search — specifically for Connecticut ground lease, reverter, and commercial forfeiture decisions — before the principle is invoked in negotiation with Pryor Whitfield & Koss, because the Authority's counsel will ask for the cite and an incorrect one would cost credibility at the moment we need it most.
@H2 6.3 The practical conclusion
@P The legal argument is sound but it should not be the strategy. An unenforceable clause is a remedy you reach after two years of litigation, during which the improvements sit in place, the construction loan is in default, and the equity is frozen. Bayside will not underwrite the reverter on the theory that a court will rescue it; it will underwrite the clause as written, or it will not close. The clause must therefore be fixed in the document, and the legal analysis should be used for two narrower purposes: to give us a credible fallback if the Authority refuses to move (we can say, in substance, that the clause as drafted is unlikely to be enforced and that the Authority should prefer a remedy it can actually obtain), and to support a savings and reformation provision in the Lease itself (Section 9.10), which converts the argument into a drafting mechanism.
@H2 6.4 Non-waivable rights and the savings clause
@P Section 9.10 and Section 16.10 of the draft are deliberate. They preserve Tenant's right to seek relief from forfeiture, and they provide that if any provision is held to operate as an unenforceable penalty the Lease shall be reformed to give effect to the parties' intent — that Tenant be paid for the Improvements and that the Authority recover only its actual, documented damages. A savings clause does two things: it improves the odds of reformation rather than invalidation, and it gives the Lender comfort that the remedy will not simply fail (leaving the Lender with a void termination and an unclear estate).
